> [!important]
> **TL;DR** — A cash CDO owns credit assets, while a synthetic securitization transfers credit risk through derivatives; both use tranches to decide who absorbs losses first.
>
> - A CDS transfers defined credit risk without requiring the protection buyer to sell the underlying bond or loan.
> - A synthetic vehicle may reference loans or bonds it never owns.
> - Synthetic tranches create exposure to default, recovery, correlation, collateral, and counterparty risk.
> - A high rating depends on the reference portfolio, attachment point, assumptions, legal terms, and counterparty support.
> - The key discipline is to separate funded cash exposure from derivative protection and then trace every payment obligation.

The word “synthetic” can sound like a product is artificial or less real. In credit markets, it usually means that the structure references credit risk through a derivative instead of buying every underlying asset. A synthetic securitization can therefore create tranches on a portfolio of corporate loans, mortgages, or bonds without transferring the entire portfolio into an SPV.

That flexibility is powerful. A bank can transfer the risk of a loan book while continuing to lend to its customers. Investors can choose a tranche of a reference portfolio. Capital can be released or redistributed without selling the underlying loans.

The same flexibility introduces another layer of risk. The investor depends not only on borrowers and recoveries but also on derivative documentation, collateral, mark-to-market mechanics, and the solvency of the protection seller.

## Foundations: credit exposure and derivatives

### A bond or loan creates credit exposure

If an investor lends \$100 to a company, the investor is exposed to the company's ability to repay. The investor may lose money if the company defaults, restructures, or becomes less valuable before maturity.

### A credit default swap

A **credit default swap**, or CDS, is a contract in which one party buys protection against defined credit events and another party sells that protection. The protection buyer pays a premium, usually called the spread. If a defined credit event occurs, the protection seller makes a settlement payment based on the loss under the contract.

The CDS does not automatically transfer ownership of the bond or loan. It transfers a contractual credit exposure.

#### Worked example: a single-name CDS

Suppose a protection buyer references \$10 million of corporate debt and pays a 400-basis-point annual premium.

1. Annual premium before day-count and payment conventions is approximately \$10 million × 4% = \$400,000.
2. If a defined default occurs and recovery is 40%, loss given default is 60%.
3. Protection settlement is approximately \$10 million × 60% = \$6 million, subject to the contract and auction process.
4. The buyer has paid premium for protection rather than selling the underlying debt.

The example is illustrative. Actual CDS payments depend on credit-event definitions, notional adjustments, settlement terms, and recovery determination.

### Cash versus synthetic CDO

A **cash CDO** owns bonds, loans, or structured securities. Investors receive cash generated by those assets.

A **synthetic CDO** references credit exposures through CDS or similar contracts. The vehicle may hold collateral for premium and settlement obligations, but it does not need to own every referenced loan.

![Cash and synthetic CDOs use different forms of collateral and credit exposure](/imgs/blogs/cdos-synthetic-securitization-credit-default-swaps-2.webp)

## 1. The reference portfolio

The reference portfolio is the set of entities or assets whose credit events determine losses. It may include corporate borrowers, mortgage bonds, loans, indices, or bespoke names.

The investor must understand whether exposure is:

- equally weighted or concentrated;
- static or managed;
- funded or unfunded;
- cash-settled or physically settled;
- subject to replacement, substitution, or termination.

### Notional is not market value

A CDS reference notional of \$100 million is not necessarily worth \$100 million. It is the amount used to calculate premium and settlement. The market value depends on spread, maturity, probability of default, recovery, liquidity, and counterparty risk.

### First-to-default and portfolio protection

A first-to-default contract pays when the first name in a basket defaults. A portfolio tranche pays after cumulative losses reach an attachment point. These are different claims on the same underlying names.

#### Worked example: reference concentration

Suppose a synthetic portfolio references 100 names with \$1 million notional each, but the top five names account for 25% of total notional.

1. The portfolio has 100 legal names.
2. A common shock to the top five can create a 25% notional concentration.
3. Name count therefore overstates diversification.
4. The tranche outcome depends on weights, correlation, and loss severity.

## 2. Synthetic tranching

Synthetic tranching divides cumulative reference-portfolio losses into attachment and detachment points. The **attachment point** is where a tranche begins absorbing losses. The **detachment point** is where the tranche is fully exhausted.

![Synthetic tranches absorb reference-portfolio losses between attachment and detachment points](/imgs/blogs/cdos-synthetic-securitization-credit-default-swaps-3.webp)

Suppose a portfolio has \$1 billion of reference notional. A tranche attaches at 3% and detaches at 7%.

- Losses below 3% do not reduce the tranche.
- Losses between 3% and 7% reduce the tranche.
- Losses above 7% fully exhaust the tranche.

The tranche thickness is 4% of the reference portfolio, or \$40 million.

#### Worked example: tranche loss

Suppose cumulative portfolio loss is 5% on a \$1 billion reference portfolio. A tranche attaches at 3% and detaches at 7%.

1. Loss below attachment: 3% × \$1 billion = \$30 million.
2. Loss reaching the tranche: 5% − 3% = 2% of reference notional.
3. Tranche loss: 2% × \$1 billion = \$20 million.
4. Maximum tranche notional: 4% × \$1 billion = \$40 million.
5. Remaining tranche protection: \$20 million.

## 3. Funded and unfunded synthetic structures

In a funded synthetic structure, investors provide collateral or purchase notes. The collateral can support the SPV's obligations to the protection buyer. In an unfunded structure, a protection seller promises to pay without first depositing the full amount, creating direct counterparty exposure.

### Collateral account

The SPV may invest collateral in high-quality assets. The collateral earns interest and can help fund premium or settlement. It also introduces investment, custody, liquidity, and reinvestment rules.

### Protection seller

The protection seller receives premium and bears loss if credit events occur. The seller may be a bank, insurer, fund, or special-purpose vehicle. The investor needs to know whether payment depends on collateral, a guarantee, margin posting, or the seller's general credit.

#### Worked example: funded protection

Suppose a bank buys \$50 million of protection from a funded SPV. Investors provide \$50 million of collateral.

1. The collateral is invested in permitted assets.
2. The bank pays premium to the SPV.
3. If reference losses reach \$8 million, the SPV uses collateral to pay the bank according to the tranche rules.
4. Investors lose the corresponding collateral value before any residual return.

The structure makes the protection obligation funded, but the collateral investment can still lose value or become illiquid.

## 4. CDS premium and settlement

The protection buyer pays periodic premium until maturity, termination, or a credit event. The premium is based on notional, spread, payment frequency, and day-count conventions.

If a credit event occurs, the contract may use physical settlement, cash settlement, or an auction. The settlement value depends on the recovery price of deliverable obligations.

### Accrued premium

Premium may accrue between payment dates. A default can trigger both a protection payment and an adjustment for accrued premium. The exact calculation is contractual.

### Credit event definitions

Bankruptcy, failure to pay, restructuring, and government intervention can have different treatment. A name can suffer severe distress without triggering the contract's defined event.

## 5. Counterparty and collateral risk

Synthetic structures add counterparty risk. The protection buyer can be right about a borrower default and still face a payment problem if the protection seller fails.

![Synthetic credit trades combine borrower default risk with protection-seller counterparty and collateral risk](/imgs/blogs/cdos-synthetic-securitization-credit-default-swaps-4.webp)

### Margin and mark-to-market

A derivative may require collateral when its mark-to-market moves. Margin reduces unsecured exposure but creates liquidity needs. A seller can face a margin call precisely when spreads widen and its portfolio loses value.

### Wrong-way risk

Wrong-way risk occurs when the protection seller is more likely to fail at the same time that the reference portfolio deteriorates. A bank selling protection on its own lending sector may have correlated exposure.

#### Worked example: counterparty stress

Suppose a protection buyer has \$20 million of expected settlement exposure to a seller after a portfolio loss. The seller has posted \$12 million of collateral.

1. Gross settlement obligation: \$20 million.
2. Posted collateral: \$12 million.
3. Uncollateralized amount: \$8 million before recovery on the seller.
4. If the seller defaults, the buyer may recover less than the contractual amount.

## 6. Correlation and tranche risk

Synthetic CDOs are highly sensitive to default correlation. If names default independently, losses may remain dispersed. If names default together, junior protection can be exhausted and senior tranches can suffer.

Correlation is not one number in the real world. It varies by industry, geography, sponsor, supply chain, macro factor, and time horizon.

### Tail concentration

A senior tranche may be protected in ordinary scenarios but exposed to a common shock. A mezzanine tranche may be most sensitive to model uncertainty because it sits where moderate losses begin to matter.

#### Worked example: independent versus clustered defaults

Suppose a portfolio has 100 names with \$1 million notional each. A 5% loss can arise in two ways.

1. Five independent defaults with 100% loss produce \$5 million loss.
2. One industry shock can also produce five defaults, but the probability of additional defaults may be higher.
3. The same current loss can imply very different future loss distributions.
4. A tranche price must reflect the path and clustering risk, not only today's cumulative loss.

## 7. CDO squared and resecuritization

A resecuritization creates claims on other structured products rather than directly on loans or bonds. A CDO squared may reference tranches of CDOs. The structure adds another layer of correlation and model dependence.

The investor must trace the underlying assets through each layer. A tranche can appear diversified because it references many securities, while those securities may all reference similar mortgages, industries, or risk factors.

![A CDO-squared tranche references first-layer CDO tranches that each reference a separate loan or bond pool, compounding correlation and model risk across two layers of resecuritization](/imgs/blogs/cdos-synthetic-securitization-credit-default-swaps-8.webp)

The post-2008 lesson is not simply that complex products are bad. It is that a second layer can concentrate uncertainty even when each first-layer pool appears diversified.

## 8. Capital relief and risk transfer

Banks may use synthetic securitization to transfer credit risk and manage regulatory capital. A bank can retain customer relationships and loan servicing while purchasing protection on a defined tranche of its portfolio.

The economic value depends on the capital treatment, premium cost, collateral, counterparty, and whether the protection is recognized under applicable rules. A transaction can reduce a bank's capital requirement without removing every economic exposure.

![Synthetic-credit uses include portfolio hedging, bank capital, tailored investment, and risk transfer](/imgs/blogs/cdos-synthetic-securitization-credit-default-swaps-6.webp)

### Risk retention

Regulatory frameworks may require a sponsor or originator to retain a meaningful interest. In a synthetic transaction, retention can be implemented through a retained tranche, a vertical slice, or other prescribed exposure. The exact requirement depends on jurisdiction and structure.

## 9. Documentation and legal definitions

CDS documentation defines the reference entity, obligations, credit events, settlement mechanism, succession, restructuring, and termination. A single word in the confirmation can affect whether a payment is triggered.

### Reference obligation

The reference entity may have several bonds and loans. The contract must specify which obligations qualify for settlement and how debt exchanges are treated.

### Succession events

Mergers, spin-offs, acquisitions, and restructurings can change the reference entity. The documentation allocates risk if a borrower is acquired, renamed, or reorganized.

### Restructuring

A maturity extension or covenant amendment may or may not qualify as a restructuring credit event. Investors should not infer contractual protection from a news headline.

## 10. Valuation of a synthetic tranche

Valuation requires projecting premium income, default losses, recoveries, collateral interest, counterparty exposure, and liquidity. The result depends on assumptions about hazard rates, recovery, correlation, dependence structure, discounting, and collateral reinvestment.

![A synthetic tranche's valuation can show a low $2 million expected loss on a $40 million notional while carrying a 5 percent chance of a $20 million tail loss](/imgs/blogs/cdos-synthetic-securitization-credit-default-swaps-9.webp)

### Expected loss is not enough

A tranche can have low expected loss but high tail risk. An investor also needs loss distribution, attachment probability, expected tranche loss, timing, and mark-to-market behavior.

#### Worked example: expected loss versus tail loss

Suppose a \$40 million tranche has an expected loss of \$2 million but a 5% chance of losing \$20 million.

1. Expected loss is \$2 million under the stated probabilities.
2. The tail scenario is a \$20 million loss.
3. An investor with leverage or redemption needs may be unable to tolerate the tail even if average economics look attractive.

## 13. Full synthetic-transaction walkthrough

Consider a bank with a \$1 billion reference portfolio. It buys protection on losses between 3% and 7% from a funded SPV. Investors fund the SPV with \$40 million of notes. The bank pays a 500-basis-point annual premium on the \$40 million tranche.

#### Worked example: synthetic tranche outcome

1. Tranche notional is \$40 million.
2. Annual premium is \$40 million × 5% = \$2 million before conventions.
3. Reference portfolio loss reaches 5% of \$1 billion, or \$50 million.
4. Loss below the 3% attachment is \$30 million.
5. Loss allocated to the tranche is \$20 million.
6. Investors retain \$20 million of tranche notional before other adjustments.

If the protection seller defaults after posting only \$10 million collateral, the bank's recovery depends on the seller and the legal process. The tranche loss and counterparty loss are separate layers.

## 14. Investor checklist

### Reference portfolio

- Who are the entities?
- What are the weights and concentrations?
- Is the portfolio static or managed?
- How are substitutions handled?

### Derivative

- What is the notional and attachment point?
- What credit events trigger payment?
- How is recovery determined?
- Is settlement cash or physical?

### Counterparty

- Who sells protection?
- What collateral and margin are required?
- Is there wrong-way risk?
- What happens after downgrade or insolvency?

### Model

- What default and recovery assumptions drive value?
- How is correlation modeled?
- What is the tail loss distribution?
- How does liquidity affect mark-to-market?

## 15. Stress matrix

| Stress | First effect | Second-order effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Defaults rise | Protection payment increases | Counterparty margin rises |
| Recoveries fall | Loss severity increases | Tranche exhaustion accelerates |
| Correlation rises | Losses cluster | Senior protection is challenged |
| Spreads widen | Mark-to-market falls | Collateral calls increase |
| Counterparty weakens | Payment uncertainty rises | Replacement becomes costly |

#### Worked example: joint synthetic stress

Suppose a \$40 million tranche has \$12 million of posted collateral. Reference losses create a \$20 million settlement obligation, and the protection seller has a 50% recovery on its uncollateralized obligation.

1. Posted collateral pays \$12 million.
2. Remaining obligation is \$8 million.
3. Expected recovery on the remaining amount is \$4 million.
4. Expected shortfall is \$4 million before timing and legal costs.

The investor must therefore model both reference loss and protection-seller solvency.

## 16. CDS basis and bond ownership

CDS protection and owning the underlying bond are related but not identical. A bond investor receives coupon and principal, has voting and restructuring rights, and faces the bond's liquidity. A CDS investor pays premium and receives a defined settlement after a credit event, but may face counterparty and documentation risk.

The spread between the bond yield and CDS spread is the basis. It can move because of funding, balance-sheet use, liquidity, deliverability, technical demand, or differences in the exact reference obligation. An investor cannot assume that buying protection on a company perfectly hedges a specific bond.

### Deliverability

Physical settlement depends on deliverable obligations. If the bond owned by a hedge fund is not eligible for delivery or has different seniority, the hedge may leave basis risk. Cash settlement uses an auction or defined price, which can differ from the holder's realized liquidation value.

#### Worked example: imperfect hedge

Suppose an investor owns \$10 million of a company's senior bond and buys \$10 million of CDS protection referencing a broader obligation set.

1. The company defaults.
2. CDS auction recovery is 45%.
3. The investor's bond can be sold for 35% because it is less liquid or structurally junior.
4. CDS protection offsets part of the loss, but the investor still has a basis difference.

## 17. Portfolio loss modeling

Synthetic tranche valuation often starts with a portfolio loss model. Each name has a default probability, recovery assumption, and exposure. A dependence model determines whether defaults occur independently or cluster.

### Single-factor intuition

A common-factor model can represent each borrower's default probability as a combination of idiosyncratic risk and a shared economic factor. When the factor deteriorates, many default probabilities rise together.

This is an abstraction for intuition. Real models can use multi-factor structures, copulas, transition matrices, hazard rates, or scenario simulations. The choice of model affects the tail loss distribution.

![Independent and clustered default scenarios can produce the same 5 percent mean portfolio loss, but a single industry shock creates a visibly fatter right tail of large losses](/imgs/blogs/cdos-synthetic-securitization-credit-default-swaps-10.webp)

### Recovery correlation

Recoveries can be correlated with defaults. In a recession, defaults rise while collateral values and enterprise values fall. Assuming a fixed recovery rate can understate tail risk if low recoveries arrive at the same time as high defaults.

#### Worked example: default and recovery dependence

Suppose a \$100 million reference portfolio has a 10% default rate.

1. At a 60% recovery rate, loss is \$4 million.
2. At a 30% recovery rate, loss is \$7 million.
3. If the low recovery occurs in the same stress scenario as the high default rate, tranche losses rise sharply.
4. Expected loss calculated using separate averages can understate joint-tail loss.

## 18. Model validation and challenger scenarios

An investor should ask how the model was calibrated, which historical periods were used, and whether the historical sample contains a stress comparable to the current portfolio. A model may be mathematically elegant but poorly calibrated to a new industry, country, or loan type.

### Challenger model

A challenger model uses different assumptions or a different dependence structure. The purpose is not to identify one “true” answer. It is to show how valuation and tranche loss move when reasonable analysts disagree.

### Reverse stress test

A reverse stress test begins with a loss threshold and asks what conditions would produce it. For a tranche that attaches at 3%, ask what combination of default rate, recovery, concentration, and correlation would reach 3% portfolio loss.

#### Worked example: reverse stress

Suppose a tranche begins losing after \$30 million of losses on a \$1 billion portfolio.

1. A 5% default rate with 40% loss severity produces 2% portfolio loss, or \$20 million.
2. A 7.5% default rate with 40% severity produces 3% loss, or \$30 million.
3. Alternatively, a 5% default rate with 60% severity also produces 3% loss.
4. The tranche can be reached by more defaults, worse recovery, or a combination.

## 19. Collateral management and margin mechanics

Funded synthetic structures hold collateral that may be invested in government securities, cash, or other permitted assets. The collateral is not a decorative buffer. Its market value, liquidity, maturity, and custody determine how much money is available when credit events occur.

### Haircuts

A collateral agreement may apply haircuts to securities. A \$100 market-value holding with a 5% haircut may receive \$95 of collateral credit. Haircuts can increase during stress, producing additional margin needs.

### Reinvestment risk

If collateral matures before the synthetic transaction, the SPV must reinvest. Falling rates can reduce collateral income. Rising rates can reduce the market value of longer collateral. The collateral portfolio therefore has its own duration and liquidity risk.

#### Worked example: haircut and margin

Suppose a protection seller posts \$15 million of government securities with a 10% haircut.

1. Market value is \$15 million.
2. Collateral credit is \$15 million × 90% = \$13.5 million.
3. If required collateral is \$14 million, the seller must post another \$500,000 or eligible cash.

## 20. Capital relief versus economic risk

A bank may use synthetic protection to reduce a regulatory capital requirement. The transaction can be economically sensible if the premium paid is lower than the benefit of capital capacity, risk reduction, or portfolio flexibility. But capital relief is not the same as eliminating the loan book's risk.

The bank may retain senior risk, servicing exposure, counterparty exposure, basis risk, or reputational incentives. A regulator may impose conditions on the protection seller, tranche thickness, documentation, or recognition of risk transfer.

### Retained senior risk

If a bank buys protection only on a mezzanine tranche, it may still retain the first-loss or senior tail. The capital result depends on which layers are transferred and how the framework recognizes them.

#### Worked example: partial risk transfer

Suppose a bank has a \$1 billion portfolio and buys protection on losses between 3% and 7%.

1. The bank still bears the first \$30 million of loss.
2. Protection covers the next \$40 million if the seller performs.
3. The bank retains losses above \$70 million.
4. The transaction transfers a defined band of risk, not the entire portfolio.

The buyer should analyze whether the platform's revenue comes from:

- recurring management fees;
- CDS execution and structuring fees;
- collateral management;
- capital-relief transactions;
- risk analytics software;
- proprietary trading or residual positions.

Each revenue stream carries different market, regulatory, and balance-sheet risk.

## 22. Synthetic-credit governance

Governance must cover model approval, counterparty limits, collateral eligibility, valuation, credit-event decisions, conflicts, and escalation. A synthetic portfolio can appear liquid because CDS are tradable, while a bespoke tranche may be difficult to unwind.

### Independent valuation

If an affiliated party provides marks, investors need a process for challenge, external quotes, and model validation. A mark affects margin, reported NAV, capital, and investor redemptions.

### Credit-event committee

The determination of whether a credit event occurred can be contentious. The committee must follow the contract and document evidence. A headline restructuring is not necessarily a CDS-triggering restructuring.

## 23. Comparison with cash securitization

| Dimension | Cash CDO | Synthetic securitization |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Underlying | Owned loans or bonds | Referenced credit exposures |
| Funding | Investor cash buys notes | May be funded or unfunded |
| Main credit link | Asset borrower | Borrower plus protection seller |
| Settlement | Asset cash flows | CDS premium and credit-event payment |
| Liquidity | Loan and security market | Derivative and tranche market |
| Key model risk | Default, recovery, timing | Correlation, recovery, counterparty |

Neither form is automatically safer. The correct choice depends on the investor's objective and capacity to understand the legal and operational chain.

## 24. Final synthetic stress walkthrough

Consider a \$1 billion reference portfolio and a \$40 million tranche attaching at 3% and detaching at 7%. The protection seller posts \$12 million collateral. A severe scenario produces 5% reference loss, a 50% recovery on uncollateralized seller exposure, and \$1 million of legal costs.

#### Worked example: combined loss and counterparty shortfall

1. Reference loss is 5% × \$1 billion = \$50 million.
2. The tranche absorbs loss between \$30 million and \$50 million, or \$20 million.
3. Posted collateral covers \$12 million.
4. Remaining contractual settlement is \$8 million.
5. At 50% recovery, expected recovery is \$4 million.
6. Legal costs reduce the economic outcome by another \$1 million.

The expected investor shortfall is approximately \$5 million before timing and other adjustments. The structure's protection was real, but it was not the same as a guaranteed \$20 million cash payment.

## 25. Reference-entity changes and M&A events

Corporate borrowers can merge, spin off divisions, refinance debt, or transfer obligations. A synthetic contract needs rules for successor entities and reference obligations. An M&A event can create a new legal entity while leaving the economic credit risk unchanged, or it can move assets and liabilities in a way that changes recovery.

### Successor risk

If a referenced company is acquired, the contract may follow the successor, the original entity, or a defined portion of the obligation. Investors should inspect the succession provisions rather than assume that a corporate name change is neutral.

### Acquisition financing

An acquisition can increase leverage, add debt ahead of existing creditors, or change guarantees. A CDS investor may have protection against a later default, but the value of that protection still depends on deliverable obligations, recovery, and the protection seller.

#### Worked example: acquisition leverage

Suppose a referenced borrower has \$200 million of debt and \$50 million of EBITDA. It acquires another company using \$100 million of new debt.

1. Original leverage is \$200 million ÷ \$50 million = 4.0 times.
2. If EBITDA does not change, post-acquisition debt is \$300 million.
3. Post-acquisition leverage becomes 6.0 times.
4. A CDS premium may widen because default probability and recovery uncertainty have changed.

## 26. Bespoke tranches and liquidity

A bespoke tranche is tailored to a particular investor's attachment, detachment, names, weights, or correlation preference. Customization can improve fit but reduces comparability and secondary-market liquidity.

### Model dependence

The price of a liquid index tranche can be observed from market transactions. A bespoke tranche may require a dealer model and assumptions about correlation, recovery, and hedging costs. Two dealers can quote materially different values without either making an arithmetic error.

### Exit risk

An investor that needs to sell may face a wide bid-ask spread or no firm bid. A tranche that appears attractive under hold-to-maturity assumptions can be unsuitable for a portfolio with daily liquidity needs.

#### Worked example: liquidity discount

Suppose a synthetic tranche has a model value of \$30 million but a stressed market bid of \$27 million.

1. Model value: \$30 million.
2. Market bid: \$27 million.
3. Liquidity and model discount: \$3 million, or 10% of model value.
4. A forced seller realizes the market value, not the model value.

## 27. Risk transfer versus risk transformation

Synthetic securitization can transfer risk from a bank to investors, but it can also transform the risk into a more concentrated form. The bank may retain the first loss and senior tail while selling the mezzanine band. Investors may acquire exposure to a portfolio through a funded note rather than a loan.

The system becomes safer only if the transferred risk is understood, priced, and supported by investors with adequate capital and liquidity. Moving risk outside a bank's balance sheet can reduce bank concentration while increasing interconnections among funds, insurers, dealers, and counterparties.

### Systemic perspective

An investor should ask who owns the same reference portfolio, who sold protection, who finances the collateral, and who may sell in a downturn. The network of exposures can matter more than the legal form of one transaction.

## 28. Bank capital and synthetic risk-transfer transactions

Banks may use credit-risk-transfer transactions to manage risk-weighted assets and capital. A transaction may cover a mezzanine tranche of a loan portfolio while the bank continues to originate, service, and monitor the loans.

The capital benefit depends on recognition rules, counterparty eligibility, documentation, attachment and detachment, maturity, and whether the protection is effective. A bank cannot assume that a CDS premium payment automatically produces capital relief.

### Retained incentives

If the bank retains the senior tranche, it still has a tail exposure. If it retains the first-loss tranche, it remains exposed to ordinary deterioration. If it retains a vertical slice, it shares all losses but may have a different incentive profile.

#### Worked example: vertical-slice retention

Suppose a bank transfers a \$100 million reference portfolio but retains 5% of every tranche.

1. The bank retains \$5 million of aggregate exposure across the structure.
2. It participates in first loss, mezzanine loss, and senior tail loss.
3. The retained amount may align incentives better than retaining only a tranche, but it does not eliminate risk.

## 29. How to read a synthetic transaction term sheet

Start with the reference portfolio and then read the derivative.

### Reference section

Identify names, weights, asset classes, maturity, substitutions, concentration limits, and default definitions.

### Tranche section

Identify notional, attachment, detachment, premium, amortization, loss allocation, and payment dates.

### Collateral section

Identify eligible collateral, haircuts, custody, reinvestment, margin, and permitted withdrawals.

### Counterparty section

Identify ratings, collateral calls, replacement rights, termination amounts, guarantees, and dispute resolution.

### Model section

Identify default probabilities, recovery assumptions, correlation, discount rate, liquidity, and stress scenarios.

This order prevents a reader from being distracted by a rating before understanding the actual cash and loss mechanics.

## 30. Final decision framework

For a synthetic credit investment, write down four numbers:

1. Expected premium income.
2. Expected reference-portfolio loss.
3. Expected counterparty and collateral shortfall.
4. Liquidity loss under forced sale.

Then write down four uncertainties:

1. Correlation and tail loss.
2. Recovery and timing.
3. Legal settlement and successor events.
4. Model and valuation disagreement.

The resulting analysis may still be uncertain, but the uncertainty is visible. That is the purpose of structured-finance analysis: not to create certainty, but to identify where the promises can fail and who bears the failure.

## 31. What changes during a market shock

In calm markets, CDS premiums are paid on schedule, collateral is easy to finance, dealers quote prices, and model disagreements remain small. During a shock, the reference portfolio loses value, spreads widen, collateral calls increase, and protection sellers may face their own funding stress. Investors can be right about long-run credit losses and still experience a near-term liquidity loss.

The shock can also change behavior. A bank may buy more protection, a fund may sell a tranche to meet redemptions, and a dealer may reduce inventory. Those actions widen prices and can make the mark-to-market loss larger than the eventual realized loss.

That is why synthetic credit needs both a hold-to-maturity analysis and a liquidity analysis. A contractual settlement is not the only path by which an investor can lose money.

The final question is always the same: if the reference names default together, the protection seller weakens, collateral loses liquidity, and the investor must sell, which layer pays and which layer absorbs the shortfall?

This question should be answered before the investment is made, not after the first margin call. It should be tested with the legal agreement, the collateral schedule, the counterparty limits, and the model outputs in the same review. Separating them creates false comfort because each individual document can appear reasonable while the combined transaction remains vulnerable.

The most useful output is therefore a map of dependencies: borrower to reference entity, reference entity to credit event, credit event to settlement, settlement to collateral, collateral to investor cash, and every handoff to a solvent party. When that map is explicit, a synthetic product becomes analyzable rather than mysterious.

It also clarifies the difference between a credit view and a funding view. The borrower may be healthy while the protection seller is weak; the reference portfolio may perform while collateral becomes illiquid; or the contract may settle correctly while a forced sale produces a loss. A complete analysis keeps all three possibilities visible.

That is why synthetic credit is not merely a bet on default. It is a contract, a collateral account, a capital stack, a model, and a network of counterparties operating at the same time.

Each layer can be priced, stressed, documented, and challenged separately.

The analysis is strongest when those answers agree.

When they do not, the disagreement is a risk signal.

Not a footnote.

It is information.

For the investor.

At every stage.

And especially during stress.

Without shortcuts.

Always.

## Common misconceptions

### “Synthetic means no real loss can occur”

The derivative creates a real payment obligation and a real loss allocation. The investor can lose funded collateral or face an unpaid protection claim.

### “CDS protection is the same as owning the bond”

CDS and bond ownership have different cash flows, legal rights, basis risk, and counterparty exposure.

### “A diversified reference portfolio is safe”

Common macroeconomic or industry factors can create correlated defaults.

### “Attachment point is the probability of loss”

Attachment describes where a tranche begins absorbing cumulative loss. It does not state how likely the portfolio is to reach that level.

### “Model precision means risk precision”

A detailed model can still be wrong if correlation, recovery, or legal assumptions are wrong.

## How it shows up in real markets

Synthetic securitization is used in bank capital management, credit-risk transfer, portfolio hedging, and structured-credit investment. It can reduce the need to sell loans and can tailor protection to a bank's risk appetite.

The benefits depend on transparent reference portfolios, enforceable documentation, adequate collateral, and counterparties able to perform. The post-crisis regulatory focus on risk retention, capital treatment, and conflicts reflects the fact that synthetic complexity can move risk without making it disappear.

## When this matters to you

Synthetic credit exposure can appear in bank capital, insurance portfolios, hedge funds, structured notes, and credit funds. You may not see a borrower loan, but the economics still depend on borrower default and recovery.

The practical workflow is:

1. Identify the reference portfolio.
2. Map attachment and detachment points.
3. Read the CDS credit-event and settlement terms.
4. Separate reference risk from counterparty risk.
5. Stress default correlation, recovery, collateral, and liquidity together.
