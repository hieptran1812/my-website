> [!important]
> **TL;DR** — "OG" is not a biography, it is a balance-sheet position. Someone matters across cycles because they hold one of three durable assets: coins at a near-zero cost basis, control of a venue or a balance sheet, or an audience that trades. Everything else is a story about the story.
>
> - **Cheap supply is the purest form of power.** A holder who paid \$3 a coin is still in profit after a 99.99% fall. They can sell into weakness that would liquidate every other participant, and they never have to explain themselves.
> - **Control is the strongest form — and the machinery outlives the owner.** BitMEX ran the full arc: charged by the CFTC on **1 October 2020** over an unregistered platform offering up to **100-to-1** leverage; a **\$100 million** penalty on **10 August 2021**; a founder's guilty plea and sentence in **2022**; the company fined **\$100 million** in **January 2025**; presidential pardons for all four founders and the company on **27 March 2025**; and a shutdown announcement on **23 July 2026**. Through every one of those, the perpetual swap it popularised around 2016 stayed the most-traded instrument in crypto — on everyone else's venues.
> - **Balance sheets transmit through forced flow, not opinion.** Grayscale's GBTC went from roughly a **+40%** premium in 2020 to nearly **−50%** in December 2022 and back to about **0%** on **11 January 2024** when it converted to an ETF — dragging the collateral behind billions of dollars of loans with it.
> - **The counter-argument is real.** Most of the 2013 and 2017 cohort is gone. We only interview the survivors, so "OG influence" is measured on a sample selected for having been right once. The parts of it that survive scrutiny are the mechanical parts — supply, control, forced flow — not the prophetic ones.
> - **The number to remember:** in the worked example below, the same 10,000 BTC fetches **\$960m** dumped on one order book and **\$998m** sliced through desks. **\$38 million** is the price of being visible — which is precisely why the largest early holders are the ones you never see trade.

Crypto reinvents its vocabulary about every eighteen months. The assets that mattered in 2017 were not the assets that mattered in 2021, and neither set is what people argue about now. The technology stack has been rewritten several times. The dominant venue has changed. The dominant narrative has changed more often than that.

And yet a strikingly small group of people keeps appearing at the centre of it. Some of them have been sued by regulators, fined into nine figures, or sentenced. Several have had their firms dissolved in bankruptcy court. A few have been out of the market entirely for years at a stretch. They keep mattering anyway.

The lazy explanation is charisma — that crypto is a personality business and these are the personalities. That explanation is wrong in an interesting way, and being precise about *why* it is wrong is the whole point of this post. Influence in this market is not a property of people. It is a property of **positions**: things you hold, control, or can reach. A person is only as durable as the position underneath them, which is why some spectacular personalities vanished with their positions and some quiet ones are still here.

![Three structurally different assets — cheap coins, control of a venue or balance sheet, and an audience — reach the price through three different channels](/imgs/blogs/the-crypto-ogs-who-they-are-and-why-they-still-matter-1.webp)

The diagram above is the mental model for this entire article, and for the five posts that follow it in this series. There are exactly three durable assets that survive a cycle: **cheap supply**, **control of a venue or a balance sheet**, and **narrative reach**. Each one reaches the price by a different route, on a different timescale, leaving a different footprint. Once you can tell them apart, most crypto news sorts itself into a supply event, a rules event, a forced-flow event, or an attention event — each of which you can reason about.

This post is the reference map. It is deliberately a taxonomy rather than six biographies, because the biographies are where people go wrong: you end up tracking whether someone is a good person instead of tracking what they are structurally able to do. Posts 32 through 36 in this series drill into individual players. This one gives you the frame to read them with.

A note on discipline before we start. Everything below that names a person or firm is either a matter of public record with its source and date attached, or is flagged as reported or alleged. Nothing here asserts that any named person or firm manipulated a market. Where allegations were contested, that is stated. This is educational writing about market structure, not investment advice and not an accusation.

## Foundations: the building blocks

If you have never traded anything, start here — nothing in this section is assumed later. If you already know what a cost basis and an order book are, skim to the next heading.

### Cost basis, and why it is the whole story

Your **cost basis** is what you actually paid for a thing. If you bought one bitcoin for \$3, your cost basis is \$3, no matter what the screen says today.

This sounds trivial. It is the single most important number in this entire post, because cost basis determines *how much pain a holder can absorb before they are forced to act*. Two people can hold the identical asset and be in completely different situations:

- The person who paid \$3 is in profit at any price above \$3. A 70% crash is an inconvenience.
- The person who paid \$100,000 an hour ago is at breakeven. A 70% crash is a catastrophe, and if they borrowed to buy, it is a liquidation.

**Drawdown tolerance** is the industry term for this: how far the price can fall before a holder is under water or under pressure. It is a function of cost basis and leverage, not of conviction. People love to explain holding through crashes as psychological strength. Usually it is arithmetic.

#### Worked example 1: what a cost basis is actually worth

Let us make this concrete with an illustrative market price of \$100,000 per BTC. (That round number is a hypothetical for the arithmetic throughout this post, not a market quote.)

Five holders, each with one coin:

| Bought | Cost basis | Value at \$100,000 | Gain | Price must fall this far before breakeven |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2011 | \$3 | \$100,000 | +3,333,233% | −99.997% |
| 2013 | \$120 | \$100,000 | +83,233% | −99.9% |
| Dec 2017 peak | \$19,000 | \$100,000 | +426% | −81% |
| Nov 2021 peak | \$69,000 | \$100,000 | +45% | −31% |
| Today | \$100,000 | \$100,000 | 0% | 0% |

Work one row by hand so the mechanism is visible. The 2011 buyer paid \$3. At \$100,000, the gain is `(\$100,000 − \$3) ÷ \$3 = 33,332.3`, or 3,333,233%. For that holder to reach breakeven, the price has to fall from \$100,000 to \$3 — a decline of `(\$100,000 − \$3) ÷ \$100,000 = 99.997%`.

Now the row that matters for market structure. Suppose the price falls 60%, to \$40,000. The 2011 holder is still up 1,333,233%. The Nov-2021 holder is down 42% and, if they used any leverage at all, has probably been closed out. **At the same price, one of these people is a comfortable seller and the other is a forced seller.** They are not making different psychological choices. They are in different arithmetic.

![The same coin is a different asset depending on when you got it — cost basis, not conviction, is what lets a holder sit through a 70% drawdown](/imgs/blogs/the-crypto-ogs-who-they-are-and-why-they-still-matter-2.webp)

*The intuition: conviction is what people call a cost basis they can afford.*

### The order book, depth, and why size is not a price

An **order book** is just two sorted lists: everyone's standing offers to buy (**bids**), highest first, and everyone's standing offers to sell (**asks**), lowest first. The gap between the best bid and the best ask is the **bid-ask spread**, the small toll you pay to trade immediately.

**Depth** is how much size is resting near the current price. A market with deep bids can absorb a large sale without the price moving much. A market with thin bids cannot: your sale eats the top bid, then the next one down, then the next, and the price you actually receive — the **average fill** — ends up well below where the screen said the market was. That shortfall is **slippage**, and it is a cost you pay to yourself in the form of a worse price.

The critical consequence, which almost everyone under-weights: **the size of a position is not the same as the money you can get for it.** A holding is worth its screen value only if you can exit at the screen price, and you usually cannot. This gap between the *mark* (what a position is carried at) and the *realisable value* (what you can actually sell it for) is the mechanism behind half the blow-ups in this article.

The companion post [how crypto prices actually move](/blog/trading/crypto-players/how-crypto-prices-actually-move) walks the order-book mechanics from zero, and [OTC desks and moving size without moving price](/blog/trading/crypto-players/otc-desks-and-moving-size-without-moving-price) covers what large sellers do instead.

### Float, supply overhang, and dormancy

The **float** is the supply actually available to trade right now — not the total number of coins in existence. Coins locked in a contract, lost to a forgotten key, or sitting in a wallet nobody has touched in a decade are not part of the float, even though they are counted in the market capitalisation.

That distinction creates **supply overhang**: coins which are not currently for sale but *could be*, at a price the current market cannot absorb. Overhang is a peculiar kind of pressure because it is invisible until it isn't. A dormant wallet exerts no selling pressure at all until the day it moves, at which point it exerts a great deal.

**Dormancy** is simply how long since a coin last moved. On-chain analytics firms track it because it is one of the few genuinely observable things about large holders: you cannot see who owns a wallet, but you can see that the wallet has not moved since 2011, and you can see the moment it does.

### What a venue actually controls

An **exchange** — or **venue** — is where the order book lives. Beginners assume an exchange is a neutral pipe. It is not. A centralised venue makes a series of decisions that determine whether an asset has a functioning market at all:

- **Listing.** Whether the asset can be traded there, and against what — a stablecoin, a major coin, or nothing.
- **Fees and rebates.** Who is paid to provide liquidity, and how much.
- **Leverage limits.** How much borrowed size a trader may take, which sets how violent forced selling becomes.
- **Collateral rules.** What assets count as margin, and at what haircut.
- **Risk labels and delisting.** Whether the asset gets flagged as risky or removed altogether.
- **Index eligibility.** Whether the asset is quoted on enough reputable venues to enter an index — which is the gateway to passive money.

Each of those is a lever that changes the price without anyone forming an opinion about value.

### What a balance-sheet owner is

The third structural asset is a **balance sheet**: a pool of other people's money that you manage, lend, or wrap, and from which you earn a fee.

Three shapes recur in crypto:

- **The wrapper.** A trust or fund that holds an asset and issues shares against it. It earns a **management fee**, a percentage of assets charged annually.
- **The lender.** Takes deposits (often from retail savers, promised a yield), lends them to trading firms, and keeps the spread.
- **The treasury.** A foundation or company sitting on a large holding of its own token plus reserves — covered in [token foundations and treasuries](/blog/trading/crypto-players/token-foundations-and-treasuries-the-on-chain-central-banks).

The reason balance sheets matter to *price* rather than just to their owners is that they generate **forced flow**: buying or selling that happens because a contract, a redemption, a margin call or a bankruptcy court requires it, not because anybody wants to trade. Forced flow is price-insensitive, and price-insensitive flow is what actually moves markets.

### Narrative reach

**Narrative reach** is an audience that acts. Not followers — *followers who trade*. It is the weakest of the three assets, the hardest to measure, and by far the most over-attributed, but it is not zero, and we will quantify what it can and cannot do later.

## 1. What actually makes someone an OG

Here is the definition this post will use, and it is deliberately unromantic:

> An OG is someone whose cost basis, control, or audience was established in a previous cycle and still functions in this one.

Three things follow from that phrasing.

**Being early is necessary but nowhere near sufficient.** Thousands of people bought bitcoin in 2011. Almost none of them matter to the market. Being early only counts if it produced a position large enough to move something, or a platform, or a following. The vast majority of early adopters bought a small amount, sold it in 2013 or 2017, and are now ordinary people with a good story.

**The asset has to still function.** A cost basis persists automatically — coins do not expire. Control does not: a venue can lose its users, a lender can go bankrupt, a fund can be wound up. An audience decays fastest of all. This is why the taxonomy is more useful than a list of names: it tells you which kinds of influence should be expected to persist and which should be expected to rot.

**Reputation is not one of the three.** This is the part that trips people up. Reputation feels like the thing OGs have. But reputation is downstream: it is what accrues to someone whose position worked. When the position stops working, the reputation goes with it, sometimes within a single week. We will see a clean example of that in the leveraged-fund section.

### The five types

![The reference map for the rest of this series: what each type of OG owns, how it reaches the price, and what takes it out](/imgs/blogs/the-crypto-ogs-who-they-are-and-why-they-still-matter-3.webp)

The three structural assets produce five recognisable types once you account for how they are held:

| Type | What they own | How it reaches price | Typical timescale | What ends it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A. Cheap-supply holder** | coins at a near-zero cost basis | supply that can arrive at any price | hours to weeks, unpredictable | the coins finally get sold |
| **B. Venue owner** | the order book and the rules | listings, fees, leverage limits, collateral rules | minutes | regulators, or the exit of trust |
| **C. Balance-sheet owner** | other people's money and a fee on it | forced buying and forced selling | weeks to months | a mark that cannot be realised |
| **D. Leveraged-conviction fund** | borrowed size and a reputation | the unwind, not the position | days | one margin call |
| **E. Narrator** | an audience and a track record | attention arriving before flow | days | being wrong in public, twice |

Read that table as the article's spine. Everything from here to the misconceptions section is an expansion of one row.

Two structural observations before we go row by row.

First, **the types compound**. The most durable players hold more than one. A venue owner who also holds a large early position in the venue's own token has both A and B. A balance-sheet owner who also writes widely-read commentary has C and E. Compounding is what turns a good position into a structural one, because the two assets defend each other: control generates the flows that make the cheap supply liquid.

Second, **the types have completely different observability**. This matters enormously for anyone trying to trade around them. Type B is loud and instantaneous — a listing announcement is a public event with a timestamp. Type A is silent until it is not. Type C is slow and, unusually, *documented*: bankruptcies and regulatory filings are public records, which is why the case studies in this post lean on them so heavily. Type E is visible but its effect is the hardest to separate from coincidence.

## 2. Type A — cheap supply: the dormant whale and the early miner

The purest form of structural power in this market belongs to people you cannot name.

An early miner who ran a laptop in 2010 and an early buyer who paid single-digit dollars hold something no amount of later capital can reproduce: coins whose cost basis rounds to zero. That gives them three properties simultaneously.

**They are never forced.** A margin call cannot reach them because they never borrowed. A drawdown cannot force them out because they are in profit at essentially any price. In a market where most participants can be made to sell against their will, being unforceable is close to being invincible.

**They can sell into any bid.** Because every price is a profit, they are indifferent to selling at \$40,000 or \$100,000 in a way that no recent buyer can be. That makes them the natural counterparty to enthusiasm: when everyone wants to buy, somebody has to be selling, and the person with no downside is the most comfortable candidate.

**They are invisible until they move.** There is no filing requirement, no disclosure threshold, no obligation of any kind. The only signal is on-chain: a wallet dormant for a decade suddenly transacts.

### The overhang is not a prediction

It is important to be precise here, because this is where commentary goes badly wrong. A dormant wallet is not evidence that a sale is coming. Most old coins that move are being reorganised — split across new wallets, moved to better custody, or transferred to heirs — not sold. A meaningful fraction of very old supply is not dormant but permanently lost: keys thrown away when the coins were worth nothing.

What the overhang does establish is a **conditional**: *if* those coins ever come to market, the market as currently constituted cannot absorb them at anything like the current price. That conditional is worth understanding precisely, because it is the same arithmetic that governs every large seller in this market.

#### Worked example 2: what selling 10,000 BTC actually costs

Suppose a holder wants to sell 10,000 BTC, and the market is at our illustrative \$100,000. On a screen, that position is worth \$1,000,000,000. Now let us find out what it is worth in cash.

**Path A — hit the bids on one venue.** Assume the book on that venue holds roughly 2,000 BTC of bids in each 2% band below the current price. That is a stylised but not unreasonable shape for a deep venue. Selling 10,000 BTC walks down five bands:

| Band | Price | Size filled | Proceeds |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | \$100,000 | 2,000 BTC | \$200,000,000 |
| 2 | \$98,000 | 2,000 BTC | \$196,000,000 |
| 3 | \$96,000 | 2,000 BTC | \$192,000,000 |
| 4 | \$94,000 | 2,000 BTC | \$188,000,000 |
| 5 | \$92,000 | 2,000 BTC | \$184,000,000 |
| **Total** | | **10,000 BTC** | **\$960,000,000** |

The average fill is `\$960,000,000 ÷ 10,000 = \$96,000` — 4% below where the market was when you started. The last print is \$92,000, an 8% fall, and it is on every chart in the world. You have also told the entire market that a very large seller exists, which is information other participants will trade against for days.

**Path B — eight OTC blocks over three weeks.** Instead, the holder goes to over-the-counter desks, which buy the block directly onto their own book and then work out of it gradually. Split the position into eight blocks of 1,250 BTC each, spread over three weeks. Assume each block is crossed at an average of 0.2% below the prevailing mid — the desk's spread plus its expected hedging cost:

`10,000 BTC × \$99,800 = \$998,000,000`

**The difference: \$998,000,000 − \$960,000,000 = \$38,000,000.**

*The intuition: \$38 million is what visibility costs, which is exactly why the biggest sellers are the ones you never see.*

One honest caveat, because it is the most common misreading of this arithmetic: **OTC does not eliminate price impact, it reschedules it.** The desk that bought your block has to sell it, and it sells into the same public market you were avoiding. What changes is the *shape* — a single 8% candle becomes several weeks of unexplained heaviness with no candle to point at. The market still absorbs the supply. It just never learns who sold, or that anyone sold at all.

### What this type explains, and what it doesn't

Type A explains why bitcoin has a persistent, structurally patient seller base that is entirely absent from the discourse. It explains why rallies into round numbers so often stall without any identifiable news. And it explains why *the absence of visible selling is not evidence of absent selling*.

It does not license the genre of commentary that reads a wallet movement as an imminent crash. The honest position is that dormant supply is a standing conditional whose timing is genuinely unknowable, and anyone claiming otherwise is selling you certainty they do not have.

## 3. Type B — the venue: owning the pipes

If cheap supply is the purest form of power, control of a venue is the strongest. A large holder can only sell what they have. A venue decides what everyone else is able to do.

![A listing is not an endorsement; it is the first step of a supply chain that ends in passive money arriving](/imgs/blogs/the-crypto-ogs-who-they-are-and-why-they-still-matter-5.webp)

The pipeline above is the mechanism. A listing decision is not an opinion about an asset — it is the first link in a supply chain. Listing determines whether market makers will commit inventory, because they will only quote where they can hedge and get paid. Committed inventory is what *creates* depth. Depth is what makes the asset eligible for indices and structured products, which is the gate through which money that never chose the asset arrives. And every step runs backwards too: a risk label or a delisting withdraws each downstream stage in order, usually faster than it was built.

That is why a listing decision is a price event before anyone has traded on the new listing. The market is not pricing an endorsement; it is pricing the arrival of the whole downstream chain.

### The public record: BitMEX

The cleanest documented example of venue power — and of what outlasts it — was BitMEX. It is worth studying now precisely because the venue is on its way out: on **23 July 2026** BitMEX published "Important Message from BitMEX," stating that it would be shutting down its platform within the next two months and asking all users to close positions and withdraw assets before the closing date; a companion notice dated **22 July 2026** delisted 35 derivatives contracts on 30 July 2026 (BitMEX blog, accessed 29 July 2026).

On **1 October 2020**, the CFTC filed a complaint in the Southern District of New York charging five entities and three individuals who owned and operated the BitMEX platform — named in the complaint as **Arthur Hayes, Ben Delo and Samuel Reed** — with operating an unregistered trading platform and violating multiple CFTC regulations, including failures of anti-money-laundering procedure (CFTC Release Number 8270-20, 1 October 2020).

The complaint's own description of the business is the useful part for our purposes. It alleged that from at least November 2014, at the direction of the founders, BitMEX offered leveraged retail commodity transactions, futures, options and swaps on cryptocurrencies including bitcoin, ether and litecoin, **allowing traders to use leverage of up to 100 to 1**. It stated that the platform had facilitated derivatives transactions with an aggregate notional value in the trillions of dollars, had received more than **\$11 billion** in bitcoin deposits, and had earned more than **\$1 billion** in fees since beginning operations in 2014. The same day, the US Attorney for the Southern District of New York indicted Hayes, Delo, Reed and Gregory Dwyer on federal charges of violating the Bank Secrecy Act and conspiracy to do so.

On **10 August 2021**, a federal court entered a consent order requiring the BitMEX entities to pay a **\$100 million civil monetary penalty**, of which up to \$50 million could be offset against a penalty assessed by FinCEN (CFTC Release Number 8412-21, 10 August 2021). BitMEX certified that as of 30 June 2021 it no longer maintained operations or business functions in the United States. FinCEN separately assessed a **\$100 million** penalty — its first enforcement action against a futures commission merchant — finding that at least **\$209 million** had been transacted with darknet markets or unregistered money services businesses, that no suspicious activity report was filed on at least **588** suspicious transactions, and that in some instances BitMEX senior leadership altered US customer information to hide a customer's true location.

Then the personal outcomes, all matters of public record. **Arthur Hayes pleaded guilty on 24 February 2022** to one Bank Secrecy Act count and was **sentenced on 20 May 2022 to six months' home detention, two years' probation and a \$10 million fine**. Delo, Reed and Dwyer also pleaded guilty and received probation. The corporate entity, HDR Global Trading, **pleaded guilty on 10 July 2024** and was **fined \$100 million on 15 January 2025**. On **27 March 2025**, President Trump pardoned all four founders and HDR Global Trading Limited.

Two pieces of fine print, stated because the honest version of this record includes them: it is not publicly confirmed whether the individual fines were paid, and it is not publicly confirmed what effect the pardons had on the financial penalties. Neither is asserted here in either direction.

Now hold that record next to a structural fact. The instrument BitMEX popularised — the **perpetual swap**, a futures contract with no expiry date, introduced to the crypto market around 2016 — became and remains the single most-traded instrument in crypto, offered by every major venue. The enforcement action reached the company and its founders. It did not reach the market structure they had created, which had already been copied everywhere.

That gap is the most important thing this section teaches, and the 2026 shutdown makes it unusually literal. **Venue power, once it changes the shape of the market, outlives the venue.** The exchange is closing. The perpetual swap it introduced, the funding rate that tethers it to spot, and the liquidation cascade that both make possible are not closing — they run the market on Binance, Bybit and OKX, and they will keep running it after BitMEX's order book goes dark. The 100-to-1 leverage the CFTC complaint described was not an eccentricity; it established the reflexive, liquidation-driven price behaviour that still characterises crypto today.

This is the single best test case in the article for the thesis, because the full arc runs the experiment for us. Charged in 2020. Convicted on a guilty plea in 2022, with a sentence and a \$10 million fine. The company convicted in 2024 and fined \$100 million in 2025. All four founders and the company pardoned in March 2025. The exchange announcing its own closure in July 2026.

At no point in that sequence did the perpetual swap stop being the most-traded instrument in crypto. Every stage of the personal and corporate story — indictment, conviction, penalty, pardon, shutdown — left the market structure exactly where it was. If OG influence were personal, a founder who was convicted and whose exchange then closed would have none left. If it is structural, what matters is whether the machinery outlives the owner, and here the machinery is not merely surviving; it is the substrate every competitor runs on. Arthur Hayes' durable contribution to this market is not a venue, a following or an opinion. It is a contract design, and contract designs cannot be fined, pardoned or wound down.

### Why leverage limits are a price lever

Here is the mechanism, and it is worth working through slowly because it explains a large fraction of crypto's violent moves.

When a trader posts \$1,000 of margin to control \$100,000 of notional exposure — 100-to-1 leverage — a 1% adverse move wipes out the margin. At that point the venue's **liquidation engine** takes over: it closes the position by selling into the open market, on the trader's behalf and without their consent, to prevent the loss exceeding the collateral.

The important part is that this seller is *price-insensitive by construction*. It is not deciding that the asset is overvalued. It is executing a rule.

#### Worked example 3: a 1% tick becomes a 10% fall

Start at \$100,000 with an illustrative book that holds roughly \$50 million of depth per 1% of price.

**Round 1.** The price ticks down 1%, to \$99,000, for some ordinary reason. Everyone at 100-to-1 leverage is now at their liquidation price. Say \$200 million of notional in that cohort. The engine market-sells \$200 million into \$50 million-per-1% depth. That is four percent of depth consumed: the price falls roughly a further 2%, to about \$97,000.

**Round 2.** At \$97,000 the market is 3% below the start, which is the liquidation distance for traders at roughly 33-to-1. Assume that cohort is larger — \$400 million of notional, because more people trade moderate leverage than extreme leverage. The engine sells \$400 million into a book the first round has already thinned. The price falls roughly another 3%, to about \$94,000.

**Round 3.** At \$94,000 the 17-to-1 cohort goes. Say \$600 million. The price falls roughly another 4%, to about \$90,000 — where the 10-to-1 cohort is now sitting at its own line.

**The result: an exogenous 1% move produced a 10% fall, and \$1.2 billion of selling that nobody chose to do.**

### The public record: Binance

The second documented case establishes a different and equally important point — that enforcement against a venue's owner is not the same as the venue losing its position.

On **21 November 2023**, Binance pleaded guilty and the US Department of Justice announced a resolution totalling **\$4,316,126,163** (DOJ press release, 21 November 2023). The components included a **\$3.4 billion** civil penalty from FinCEN with a five-year monitorship, and **\$968,618,825** from OFAC for 1,667,153 apparent violations between August 2017 and October 2022. A separate CFTC settlement was valued at **\$2.7 billion**. **Changpeng Zhao** pleaded guilty to one Bank Secrecy Act count, was fined **\$50 million** personally, stepped down as chief executive, and was sentenced on **30 April 2024** to four months. He was pardoned on **23 October 2025**.

Set against that, the venue's market position. Binance's share of centralised spot volume peaked at **55.2% in January 2023**, fell to **30.1% by December 2023**, reached a four-year low near **27% in October 2024**, and then settled around a third — **34.0% in January 2026** and **35.4% in March 2026** (CCData).

Read those two paragraphs together and the structural lesson is unmissable. The largest financial penalty in the sector's history, a guilty plea and a custodial sentence for the founder coincided with the venue's share falling by roughly half — and then recovering to about a third of the entire market. **Control degraded. It did not transfer.** For a fuller treatment of this specific venue, see [Binance: the everything exchange and its gravity](/blog/trading/crypto-players/binance-the-everything-exchange-and-its-gravity) and the broader [centralised exchanges](/blog/trading/crypto/centralized-crypto-exchanges-binance-coinbase) primer.

### What ends a venue owner

Two things, and neither is usually the regulator. The first is **the exit of trust** — a solvency scare, real or perceived, which is what removed FTX from the board in a matter of days ([the FTX collapse](/blog/trading/crypto/ftx-collapse-sam-bankman-fried) is the case study). The second is **irrelevance**: users migrating for better fees, better products or better jurisdictions, which is the slower death BitMEX ultimately met — it survived the \$100 million penalty by five years and then wound the platform down anyway. Fines are survivable. A withdrawal queue is not, and neither, eventually, is being the venue everyone learned from and then left.

## 4. Type C — the balance sheet: fees, marks and forced flow

The third type is the one that generates the most *documented* price impact, because balance sheets leave paper. When a lender fails, the failure is adjudicated in a court with a public docket. This is why the case studies here are unusually well-evidenced compared to everything else in crypto.

The core insight for this type is that **a balance-sheet owner does not need to have a view on price to move it.** They move it because contracts oblige them to.

### The wrapper: how a trust becomes a price mechanism

Start with the simplest possible structure. A trust holds bitcoin and issues shares against it. Each share represents a fixed slice of the bitcoin inside. The **net asset value** (NAV) per share is simply the bitcoin held divided by shares outstanding, valued at the market price.

Here is the thing that makes wrappers interesting, and it is the single most under-appreciated mechanism in this article: **the share price and the NAV are two different prices, set by two different markets.** The NAV is set by the bitcoin market. The share price is set by whoever is buying and selling shares. If those two prices diverge, the wrapper trades at a **premium** (share price above NAV) or a **discount** (share price below NAV).

The historically important instance is Grayscale's Bitcoin Trust, GBTC, which charged a **2% annual management fee** on the assets it held.

![The wrapper, not the bitcoin, was the trade — and the wrapper is what broke](/imgs/blogs/the-crypto-ogs-who-they-are-and-why-they-still-matter-6.webp)

The documented path of that premium is the spine of an entire era of crypto credit:

- GBTC traded at a **premium** — roughly 10% to about 40% — through late 2020 and into early 2021.
- The premium **flipped to a discount in late February 2021** (CoinDesk).
- The discount widened through 2022: about **−36% on 30 September 2022** (CoinDesk), then to a record of nearly **−50% in December 2022**, after the FTX collapse.
- It stayed deeply negative until **11 January 2024**, when GBTC converted into a spot bitcoin ETF, its fee dropped from 2% to 1.5%, and the discount closed to roughly **0%** for the first time since February 2021 (CoinDesk; Bloomberg).

#### Worked example 4: the premium trade, and the same trade in reverse

**The good years.** Suppose you deposit **\$1,000,000** of bitcoin with the trust and receive shares worth \$1,000,000 at NAV. Six months later the shares trade at a **40% premium**. You sell:

`\$1,000,000 × 1.40 = \$1,400,000`

Your gross gain from the premium alone is **\$400,000** — a 40% return in six months, before counting anything bitcoin did. This is not a view on bitcoin. It is a view on the wrapper, and while it worked it was close to free money, which is why so much capital piled into it that the trust's size visibly ballooned.

**The reversal.** Now run the identical mechanics at a **35% discount**. You deposited \$1,000,000 of bitcoin; the shares you are now permitted to sell fetch:

`\$1,000,000 × 0.65 = \$650,000`

You have lost **\$350,000** on the wrapper, and — this is the part that surprises people — **that loss is independent of what bitcoin did.** If bitcoin doubled while your discount widened to 35%, your NAV is \$2,000,000 and your shares fetch \$1,300,000. You were right about the asset and still lost \$700,000 against the mark, purely on the container you bought it through.

*The intuition: when you buy an asset through a wrapper, you have bought two things, and the second one has its own market.*

### Why a discount becomes everybody's problem

A wrapper's discount would be a private disappointment if the shares were only ever held outright. They were not. They were pledged as **collateral** — posted against loans, and carried on lenders' books at the mark.

![Everyone in the chain was covered at the mark and underwater at the exit](/imgs/blogs/the-crypto-ogs-who-they-are-and-why-they-still-matter-7.webp)

#### Worked example 5: the collateral gap

Take the documented shape of the 2022 failure. Reporting based on court filings put the crypto lender Genesis's exposure to the hedge fund Three Arrows Capital at about **\$2.36 billion** (The Block, July 2022), backed partly by collateral including roughly **17.4 million GBTC shares** plus other trust shares and tokens.

Work the two columns.

**On the books, at the mark.** The loan is \$2.36bn. The collateral is carried at net asset value. On that basis the position looks fully covered: a risk system reports collateral roughly equal to or above the loan, and the loan is fine.

**At the exit, in the market.** The collateral can only be sold at the traded share price, which by late 2022 was approaching a 50% discount to NAV. Apply a 45% discount to a collateral pool marked at, say, \$2.4bn:

`\$2,400,000,000 × (1 − 0.45) = \$1,320,000,000`

The recoverable value has fallen by roughly **\$1.08 billion** while the loan has not moved by a cent. Genesis subsequently filed a claim of about **\$1.2 billion** against the 3AC estate (CoinDesk, 18 July 2022). Three Arrows Capital filed for bankruptcy on **1 July 2022**.

*The intuition: assets are volatile and debts are fixed, and a mark is only a real number if someone will pay it.*

Notice what happened to the price of bitcoin in that chain. Nobody in it wanted to sell bitcoin. A discount widened, collateral values fell, margin calls went out, and positions were liquidated into the market. **Forced flow, generated entirely inside a balance sheet.**

### The rest of the public record

The consequences ran on for years and are unusually well documented, which makes this the best-evidenced episode in the whole taxonomy:

- Genesis filed for bankruptcy in **January 2023**.
- The SEC charged Genesis and Gemini on **12 January 2023** over the Gemini Earn programme, which the SEC described as raising roughly **\$900 million from about 340,000 Earn investors**; Genesis agreed to pay a **\$21 million** penalty to settle (SEC, 2024).
- The New York Attorney General sued Gemini, Genesis and Digital Currency Group on **19 October 2023**, alleging fraud affecting 230,000-plus investors and exceeding \$1 billion; the NYAG later secured a settlement **worth \$2 billion** from Genesis (May 2024).
- Under a separate settlement announced **28 February 2024**, Gemini agreed to return at least **\$1.1 billion** to Earn users and pay a **\$37 million** fine.
- Gemini sued Genesis in **October 2023** over roughly **30.9 million GBTC shares**, collateral it valued at about **\$1.6 billion** (CoinDesk, October 2023) — the same mark-versus-exit gap, litigated.

The allegations against Digital Currency Group and its founder were contested and publicly denied; they are stated here as allegations, which is what the filings make them. What is *not* contested, and what matters structurally, is the mechanism: a wrapper's discount propagated into collateral values, collateral values into margin calls, and margin calls into forced selling. That chain is described more fully in [Pantera, DCG and the crypto conglomerates](/blog/trading/crypto-players/pantera-dcg-and-the-crypto-conglomerates).

## 5. Type D — leveraged conviction: when the unwind is the event

The fourth type looks like the third but behaves completely differently, and conflating them is a common analytical error.

A leveraged fund's influence on price is almost entirely **negative and terminal**. While the position is on, it is one buyer among many and the market barely notices. The market event is the **unwind** — and the unwind happens all at once, at the worst possible moment, in the assets everyone else also holds.

Three Arrows Capital is the canonical instance. Founded in **2012** by Su Zhu and Kyle Davies, it ran a quiet arbitrage strategy in emerging-market currencies before moving into crypto, and by the 2021 bull market had an estimated **\$10 billion** in assets under management at its peak. When Terra's UST stablecoin lost its peg in **May 2022** and the broader market fell, its concentrated, leveraged bets detonated; it defaulted on roughly **\$3.5 billion** and filed for bankruptcy on **1 July 2022**. The full contagion chain — through Celsius, Voyager, BlockFi and Genesis to ordinary depositors — is the subject of [Three Arrows Capital and crypto-lender contagion](/blog/trading/crypto/three-arrows-capital-and-crypto-lender-contagion), and the shock that lit the fuse is covered in [the Terra/LUNA collapse](/blog/trading/crypto/terra-luna-2022-collapse).

Three structural features of this type are worth extracting, because they generalise well beyond one fund.

**The reputation was the collateral.** Lenders extended billions with little or no security, on the strength of a track record. This is the clearest possible demonstration that reputation is not a fourth structural asset but a *substitute* for one — and a substitute that evaporates precisely when it is needed. When the fund could not meet margin calls, there was no collateral to seize and no code to liquidate the position.

**Correlated positions are one position.** The fund's bets were consensus trades, held simultaneously across the industry. When one fell, they all fell, at the same firms, which were also each other's counterparties. Diversification across three assets that go to zero together is not diversification.

**The unwind is bigger than the position.** A \$10 billion book does not sell \$10 billion of assets when it fails. It triggers margin calls at every lender it borrowed from, each of which liquidates its own collateral, each of which pushes the price down and triggers the next call. This is the same reflexive loop as the liquidation cascade in worked example 3, running on a monthly rather than a minute-by-minute clock.

**What ends this type is arithmetic, and it ends it permanently.** Unlike a venue owner, who can absorb a fine, or a cheap-supply holder, who cannot be forced, a leveraged fund has exactly one life. This is the strongest evidence in the whole taxonomy that "OG" status is positional rather than personal: the same people, the same reputation, the same market access — and once the position was gone, the influence was gone with it.

## 6. Type E — narrative reach: attention as an instrument

The fifth type is the one crypto talks about most and understands least.

Some early figures have genuinely large audiences: long-running blogs, essays, podcasts, feeds followed by people who trade. The claim that this constitutes market power is made constantly, usually without any attempt at quantification. Let us attempt one, because the arithmetic is clarifying in both directions.

#### Worked example 6: from an audience to a flow

Suppose a well-known commentator publishes a thesis on a token with a \$500 million market capitalisation and roughly \$20 million of daily volume.

Start with the funnel:

- **Audience:** 500,000 people see the piece within 48 hours.
- **Read it properly:** 10% → 50,000 people.
- **Able and willing to act on it:** 5% of those → 2,500 people. (Most readers hold no account with the right venue, no spare capital, or no interest.)
- **Average ticket:** \$4,000.

`2,500 × \$4,000 = \$10,000,000 of buying`

Ten million dollars against \$20 million of daily volume is **50% of a day's volume**, arriving over roughly two days. On a \$500m token with thin depth, that is easily a double-digit percentage move.

Now change one input. Run the identical funnel against bitcoin. The same \$10 million of retail buying lands in a market whose daily spot volume runs in the tens of billions. It is a rounding error — well under a basis point of the day's turnover, invisible against ordinary noise.

*The intuition: narrative reach is not a fixed quantity of power; it is a fixed quantity of flow, and whether that flow matters depends entirely on what it is pointed at.*

That single comparison dissolves most of the confusion around this type. Narrative reach is a genuine, mechanical force in small and mid-cap tokens with thin books, and it is essentially nil in the majors. Anyone attributing a bitcoin move to a commentator's essay is making a claim the arithmetic does not support.

Three honest qualifications belong here, and they all cut against over-crediting this channel.

**Attribution is nearly impossible.** When a token rises after a prominent post, the post is one of hundreds of things that happened. Commentators are also *selected for* writing about assets already gathering momentum, which manufactures a correlation with no causal content.

**Disclosure changes the reading.** Some of the most-read crypto commentators run funds and disclose positions in the assets they write about. Read as commentary, a disclosed position looks like a conflict; read as a *signal*, it is the opposite — it is the most honest thing on the page, because it tells you the writer is on one side of the trade you are about to take. The material question is never whether someone is talking their book; almost everyone is. It is whether they told you.

**Reach decays fastest of the three assets.** Coins do not expire and venues take years to lose. An audience can be lost with two consecutive bad calls.

## 7. How it shows up in price

Pull the five types back together into what a reader can actually use: four distinct transmission channels, each with its own footprint, warning time and counterparty.

![The defensive version of the taxonomy: what is visible before the move, and what is only visible after](/imgs/blogs/the-crypto-ogs-who-they-are-and-why-they-still-matter-8.webp)

| Channel | The signal you can see | Warning you get | Who is selling to you |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Dormant supply wakes** | old coins move to an exchange or a desk | hours to weeks | a holder with no downside at any price |
| **A venue changes a rule** | a listing, a risk label, a leverage cap, a collateral change | minutes, if that | market makers pulling depth |
| **A balance sheet is forced** | a persistent discount to NAV, a withdrawal halt, a court filing | weeks to months | a lender who has no choice |
| **A narrative arrives** | a post, then volume before price | days | whoever positioned before the post |

Four observations that make this table worth more than the sum of its rows.

**The best-telegraphed channel is the one nobody watches.** Balance-sheet failures give *months* of warning. A wrapper trading at a persistent 40% discount is a public number. A withdrawal halt is an announcement. A bankruptcy docket is a public record. In 2022 every stage of that chain was visible in advance to anyone reading filings instead of feeds. It is the slowest, loudest, most legible channel in crypto, and it is the one retail consistently learns about from the news after the fact.

**The fastest channel is the one you cannot trade.** Venue rule changes give effectively no warning, and by the time you read the announcement, the market makers whose systems parse it in milliseconds have already repriced. There is no edge available here. The correct response is not speed, it is *position sizing* — not being in a state where a rule change can hurt you badly.

**Only one channel has a natural counterparty.** In three of the four rows, the person selling to you is under some form of compulsion or has no downside. That asymmetry is the actual content of this article: when you buy in these moments, you are usually trading against someone with a structurally better position than yours, and no amount of conviction alters that.

**The channels compound in one direction.** They tend to fire together in sell-offs and separately in rallies. A forced balance sheet sells, the price falls, leveraged positions liquidate, dormant holders take the opportunity to lighten into the resulting volume, and the narrative turns bearish. Rallies rarely produce a corresponding synchronised buy. This asymmetry is a large part of why crypto drawdowns are faster than crypto rallies.

## The honest counter-argument: how much of this is survivorship?

An article like this owes you the strongest available case against its own thesis, so here it is — and parts of it are correct.

### The case against "OG influence"

**We only ever interview the survivors.** The 2013 and 2017 cohorts contained thousands of people with early positions, exchanges, funds and followings. The overwhelming majority are gone: they sold too early, got hacked, ran an exchange that failed, or made one leveraged bet that ended them. The "OGs" are the residue of a brutal filter. Studying them to learn what produces influence is like studying lottery winners to learn how to pick numbers — the sample is defined by the outcome you are trying to explain.

**Being right once is not skill.** In an asset that rose by orders of magnitude over a decade, buying early and not selling produced spectacular returns for reasons that had nothing to do with insight. Many early holders were simply not paying attention, which turned out to be the optimal strategy. The subsequent reputation for foresight is retrofitted onto a hold that was frequently accidental.

**The narrative machine has an appetite.** Crypto media needs recurring characters, and "OG" is a title conferred by repetition rather than earned by any measurable criterion. The more someone is quoted, the more of an OG they become, and the more they are quoted. That loop generates apparent significance out of nothing but attention.

**The base rate of blowing up is high.** Several of the figures most often described as OGs presided over firms that went bankrupt, were fined into nine figures, or both. If the category tracked genuine skill you would expect the survival rate to be better than the market's, and it is not obviously better at all.

**Concentration is falling.** As spot ETFs, listed companies and institutional allocators have entered, the share of the market's capital controlled by individuals with pre-2015 positions has fallen mechanically, whatever those individuals do.

### What survives that critique

The critique lands squarely on the *mystical* version of the claim — the version where early figures have insight, foresight, or a read on the market that others lack. That version deserves the demolition and should be discarded entirely.

But look at what the critique cannot touch. It says nothing about the arithmetic of a \$3 cost basis. It says nothing about who decides what gets listed and at what leverage. It says nothing about whether a lender's collateral can be sold at its mark. Those are not narratives; they are properties of positions, and they hold whether or not the person holding them is wise, lucky, or currently in the news.

So the honest formulation is a split:

> Their **judgment** is a survivorship artefact and should be weighted accordingly — which is to say, near zero. Their **positions** are real, mechanical and worth tracking, and would still be worth tracking if the people holding them were anonymous.

This is a genuinely useful distinction to carry around, because it tells you what to do with any given piece of OG-related news. A prominent early figure's *opinion* about where the market is going carries approximately the weight of a stranger's. The same figure's *disclosed sale*, or their venue's change to collateral rules, is a mechanical fact about supply or market structure and deserves your full attention.

There is also a testable version of the sceptical claim, which is worth stating because falsifiability is rare in this discourse. If OG influence were purely narrative, you would expect their public statements to predict returns and their structural actions not to. The mechanism described in this article predicts the reverse: statements should be noise, while listings, leverage changes, collateral rule changes and forced-flow events should show measurable, repeatable impact. That is not a settled empirical question, but it is at least a question with an answer, and it is the right one to ask.

## Common misconceptions

**"OGs coordinate."** The mechanisms in this article require no coordination whatsoever and are fully explained without it. Cheap-supply holders sell into strength because the arithmetic favours it. Venues change rules because risk management requires it. Balance sheets liquidate because contracts oblige them to. Simultaneous behaviour by similarly-positioned actors responding to the same prices looks exactly like coordination and is not. Assuming a conspiracy where structure suffices makes you worse at predicting the next move, because structure is knowable in advance and conspiracies are not.

**"A whale wallet moving means a dump is coming."** Old coins move for custody upgrades, wallet reorganisation, inheritance, lending arrangements and exchange migrations far more often than for sales. The move is the *only* observable, which is why it gets over-interpreted. Treat it as a small increase in the probability of supply, not as a forecast.

**"They got rich because they understood the technology."** Some did. Many held through the entire period without following developments at all, and inattention was frequently the superior strategy — it prevented the selling that conviction would have rationalised. Correlation between early wealth and technical insight is much weaker than the interview circuit suggests.

**"Regulators removed them."** The public record shows the opposite pattern. Substantial penalties, guilty pleas and custodial sentences are matters of record for several major figures, and the associated platforms and market structures largely persisted. Enforcement reaches people and companies; it rarely reaches positions, and almost never reaches market structure that has already been copied.

**"An OG's public call moves the market."** In a \$500 million token with a thin book, worked example 6 shows a plausible flow of \$10 million — genuinely material. In bitcoin, the same flow is a rounding error against daily turnover. The claim is size-dependent, and it is almost always stated without reference to size, which is what makes it wrong most of the time it is made.

**"Being early is the whole thing."** Being early produced the cost basis, but a cost basis on a small position gives you a good story and no influence. What makes someone structurally significant is *size*, *control*, or *reach* — and those were mostly built afterwards, with the capital that being early provided.

## How it shows up in real markets

Five documented episodes, each one a clean instance of a single row of the taxonomy.

### 1. The wrapper that became the trade (2020–2024)

GBTC traded at a premium of roughly 10% to about 40% through late 2020 and into early 2021, then flipped to a discount in late February 2021 (CoinDesk). The discount reached about −36% on 30 September 2022 (CoinDesk) and a record of nearly −50% in December 2022, before closing to roughly 0% on 11 January 2024 when the trust converted into a spot bitcoin ETF and its fee fell from 2% to 1.5% (CoinDesk; Bloomberg).

The mechanism from this post, running in public: a balance-sheet owner earning a fee on assets it held, with a share price set in a different market from the assets themselves. While the premium was positive it manufactured demand for bitcoin, because creating shares required delivering bitcoin. When it inverted, it manufactured distress in everything that had been collateralised against those shares. The lesson is that a wrapper is a *second asset* with its own supply and demand, and its dislocation can be larger and longer-lasting than anything happening in the underlying.

### 2. The collateral that could not be sold (2022)

Reporting based on court filings put Genesis's exposure to Three Arrows Capital at about \$2.36 billion (The Block, July 2022), backed partly by roughly 17.4 million GBTC shares plus other trust shares and tokens. When 3AC could not meet a margin call, Genesis liquidated the collateral, sent a notice of default, and later filed a claim of about \$1.2 billion against 3AC's estate (CoinDesk, 18 July 2022). 3AC filed for bankruptcy on 1 July 2022; Genesis filed in January 2023.

This is worked example 5 as a matter of record. Every participant was solvent at the mark and insolvent at the exit, and the gap between those two numbers was the discount on a wrapper. Note also that Gemini's October 2023 suit against Genesis concerned roughly 30.9 million GBTC shares valued at about \$1.6 billion (CoinDesk, October 2023) — the same collateral, the same gap, litigated between the survivors.

### 3. The venue whose structure outlived the venue (2020–2026)

On 1 October 2020 the CFTC charged five entities and three individual owners of BitMEX with operating an unregistered trading platform and AML failures, alleging leverage of up to 100 to 1 offered since November 2014, more than \$11 billion in bitcoin deposits and over \$1 billion in fees (CFTC Release 8270-20). The same day the US Attorney for SDNY indicted four individuals on Bank Secrecy Act charges. On 10 August 2021 a consent order imposed a \$100 million civil monetary penalty on the entities (CFTC Release 8412-21), and BitMEX certified it had ceased US operations as of 30 June 2021.

FinCEN separately assessed \$100 million — its first action against a futures commission merchant — citing at least \$209 million transacted with darknet markets or unregistered money services businesses and no suspicious activity report on at least 588 suspicious transactions. Arthur Hayes pleaded guilty on 24 February 2022 and was sentenced on 20 May 2022 to six months' home detention, two years' probation and a \$10 million fine; Delo, Reed and Dwyer pleaded guilty and received probation. HDR Global Trading pleaded guilty on 10 July 2024 and was fined \$100 million on 15 January 2025. On 27 March 2025 President Trump pardoned all four founders and HDR Global Trading Limited.

Then the venue itself reached the end. On 23 July 2026 BitMEX published "Important Message from BitMEX," saying it would shut down its platform within the next two months and asking users to close positions and withdraw assets before the closing date; a notice of 22 July 2026 delisted 35 derivatives contracts on 30 July 2026 (BitMEX blog, accessed 29 July 2026).

The taxonomy's point is what did *not* change. The perpetual swap, popularised on that platform around 2016, is the dominant instrument in crypto and is offered by every major venue. Enforcement reached the owners and the company; the shutdown reached the order book. Neither reached the market structure, which had propagated beyond the reach of any single action years earlier. This is the strongest available evidence for the article's thesis: the venue was the *asset*, and when the asset expired the influence attached to it expired too — while the contract design, which was never anyone's property, simply kept running on everybody else's servers.

### 4. The plea that did not transfer control (2023–2026)

On 21 November 2023, Binance pleaded guilty and DOJ announced a \$4,316,126,163 resolution, including \$3.4 billion from FinCEN with a five-year monitorship and \$968,618,825 from OFAC for 1,667,153 apparent violations. CZ pleaded guilty to one Bank Secrecy Act count, was fined \$50 million personally, stepped down as CEO, was sentenced on 30 April 2024 to four months, and was pardoned on 23 October 2025.

Across that period the venue's share of centralised spot volume went from a 55.2% peak in January 2023 to 30.1% in December 2023, a four-year low near 27% in October 2024, and back to 34.0% in January 2026 and 35.4% in March 2026 (CCData). Control degraded by roughly half and then partly recovered. It did not move to a competitor in any durable way. For a market-structure reader, the number that matters is not the fine; it is the share.

### 5. The unwind that was larger than the fund (2022)

Three Arrows Capital, founded in 2012 and running an estimated \$10 billion at its peak, defaulted on roughly \$3.5 billion and filed for bankruptcy on 1 July 2022 after concentrated leveraged positions collapsed in the Terra/LUNA sell-off.

Two lessons that generalise. First, the market impact of a leveraged fund is concentrated almost entirely in its failure — a fund of that size buying over two years was unremarkable, and the same fund unwinding over three weeks was a systemic event. Second, this is the cleanest available refutation of the personality theory of influence: the people, reputations and relationships were unchanged on 2 July 2022, and the influence was gone, because the position was.

## If you are on the other side of the trade

The practical value of a taxonomy is that it tells you what to do differently. This is educational, not advice, and none of it is a recommendation to buy or sell anything — but each item follows directly from a mechanism above.

**Assume the seller has a better cost basis than you.** In any rally, the natural sellers are the holders for whom every price is a profit. You cannot avoid this — someone has to be on the other side — but you can stop interpreting a stalling rally as a personal failure of timing. It is the structure of the market you are in.

**Read filings, not feeds.** The balance-sheet channel is the best-telegraphed and most damaging of the four. Discounts to NAV, withdrawal halts, bankruptcy dockets, regulatory settlements and enforcement releases are public, dated and free. In 2022 they gave months of warning. Almost nobody read them; almost everybody read commentary about them, weeks later.

**Treat a discount to NAV as a risk signal, not a bargain.** A wrapper trading well below the assets it holds looks like buying a dollar for sixty cents. It is more reliably a signal that something structural prevents redemption — and that everyone holding it as collateral is about to have a problem. The discount is information about the wrapper's plumbing, not a free lunch.

**Size for the rule change you did not see coming.** You cannot react to a leverage cap, a collateral change or a risk label faster than the systems that parse them automatically. What you can control is whether such an event is survivable. That is a position-sizing decision, made in advance, and it is the only defence that actually works against the venue channel.

**Respect what leverage does to the seller pool.** Worked example 3 shows a 1% move becoming a 10% fall with no seller who wanted to sell. If you are leveraged, you are not merely exposed to the asset; you are exposed to the venue's liquidation logic and to everybody else's leverage. The cascade does not care what you think about the asset.

**Separate the disclosed position from the opinion.** When someone with reach writes about a token, the disclosure is the informative part. The argument is what they want you to believe; the position is what they have actually done. Weight accordingly, and prefer writers who tell you.

**Watch what they do structurally, not what they say directionally.** This is the whole article in one line. An early figure's market view is a survivorship artefact. Their venue's collateral rules, their trust's discount, their fund's margin position and their coins' movement are mechanical facts about supply and structure. One of those categories is worth your attention and it is not the one that gets quoted.
