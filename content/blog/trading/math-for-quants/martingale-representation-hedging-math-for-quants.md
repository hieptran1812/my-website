> [!important]
> **TL;DR:** Delta hedging is not a clever construction someone invented. In a complete market it is the only thing a claim could possibly be, and a theorem says so.
>
> - The **martingale representation theorem** says that any martingale living on a filtration generated by a Brownian motion can be written as a constant plus a stochastic integral against that Brownian motion, and the integrand is **unique**.
> - Under the risk-neutral measure the discounted price of a claim *is* such a martingale. So a unique integrand exists, and after one division that integrand is the number of shares you hold. The hedge is forced, not designed.
> - **Uniqueness is the load-bearing half.** Existence gives you a hedge; uniqueness is why the claim has a price rather than a range of prices.
> - Add one source of randomness the stock does not span and it all collapses. On an illustrative \$50m book, the same claim is worth anywhere from \$0 to \$2,500,000, and both ends are arbitrage-free.
> - The theorem says the integrand exists. It does not say you can compute it or trade it. On a \$50m short call, rebalancing 63 times leaves \$221,695 of hedging error, and buying that down to \$55,424 costs \$236,250 in spread.

## Two answers to the same question

Ask a candidate why delta hedging works and you get one of two answers.

The first is the recipe. Black-Scholes gives you a price, you differentiate it with respect to the stock, and the derivative tells you how many shares to hold. It is correct, it is what most desks type into a risk system, and it explains nothing. Differentiate *why*? And what do you do for a claim with no closed-form price, or one whose payoff depends on the whole path, so there is no function of today's stock price to differentiate at all?

The second starts at a theorem about martingales that appears to have nothing to do with options, and arrives at the same number with an explanation attached. That is the martingale representation theorem, and it is the reason replication works.

The figure above lays the two chains side by side. The recipe tells you what to hold; the theorem tells you why nothing else could have worked, and that difference is the whole post.

Every dollar figure below is illustrative arithmetic on assumed inputs, chosen so a reader can check it, not a quoted market price. The sources at the end are for the mathematics.

## Foundations: the building blocks

Five terms, each from zero. If you have them, skim.

A **contingent claim** is any contract whose payoff depends on how the world turns out: a call paying whatever the stock exceeds \$100 by, a bet paying \$1,000,000 if an index never touches a barrier, an exotic paying the average price over a year. Mathematically one object, a number $X$ not known today but known at time $T$.

**Brownian motion** $W$ is the standard model of accumulated noise, a continuous path from zero with independent increments whose variance grows linearly in time, built from a random walk by shrinking the steps: [Brownian motion from the random walk](/blog/trading/math-for-quants/brownian-motion-random-walk-math-for-quants).

A **filtration** $\{\mathcal{F}_t\}$ is the record of what is knowable at each moment, and it only grows: [Filtrations, adapted processes, and no look-ahead](/blog/trading/math-for-quants/filtrations-no-lookahead-math-for-quants). A process is **adapted** when its value at time $t$ is decidable from $\mathcal{F}_t$, which for a position means you size it on what has happened, not on what will.

A **martingale** is a process whose best forecast of its own future is its present value: $E[M_T \mid \mathcal{F}_t] = M_t$. A fair game. It can wander enormously; what it cannot do is drift in a direction you could have predicted.

Finally, the **stochastic integral** $\int_0^t H_s \, dW_s$, built in [the Ito integral and Ito's lemma](/blog/trading/math-for-quants/ito-integral-itos-lemma-math-for-quants). Read it as a trading P&L: hold $H_s$ units of something whose price moves like $W$, and this is what you have made by time $t$. Because $W$ has no drift, the integral has none either, whatever adapted $H$ you pick.

### What "generated by" actually means

Everyone repeats this phrase and few unpack it, and the whole theorem turns on it.

The filtration **generated by** $W$, written $\mathcal{F}_t$, is every event whose truth you could settle by watching the path of $W$ from time 0 to time $t$ and nothing else. "Did $W$ cross 0.5 before time $t$?" is in it. "Will $W_T$ be positive?" is not, because you cannot settle it yet. Nor is "did the central bank cut rates this morning?", unless that cut moved $W$.

The operational version: a random variable is measurable with respect to $\mathcal{F}_T$ exactly when it is some function, however complicated, of the path of $W$ from 0 to $T$. A world whose filtration is generated by one Brownian motion has exactly one source of surprise, and everything uncertain in it is a function of that one wiggling path.

## The theorem, stated precisely

Let $W$ be a Brownian motion, let $\{\mathcal{F}_t\}$ be the filtration it generates, and let $M$ be a martingale with respect to that filtration satisfying $E[M_T^2] \lt \infty$. Then there is an adapted process $H$ with $E\left[\int_0^T H_s^2 \, ds\right] \lt \infty$ such that

$$
M_t = M_0 + \int_0^t H_s \, dW_s, \qquad 0 \le t \le T,
$$

with probability one, and $H$ is unique: any other process with the same property agrees with $H$ except on a set of times and states of total measure zero.

![Pipeline showing any square integrable martingale on a Brownian filtration entering the martingale representation theorem and leaving as a constant plus a stochastic integral, with existence and uniqueness annotations and a caution that it fails with a second source of randomness](/imgs/blogs/martingale-representation-hedging-math-for-quants-2.webp)

That figure is the theorem as a machine. A martingale goes in, one integrand comes out, and the two annotations on the output are the payload: the integrand exists for every such martingale, and there is only one of it.

Read it backwards and it sounds absurd. Every fair game on this filtration, however baroque, is a trading strategy in $W$. Not approximately, not in distribution: exactly, path by path. The average price over a year, the maximum, the first barrier crossing. All of them, once you subtract the constant, are $\int H \, dW$.

## Why existence and uniqueness is the real content

The content is the two words in front of the formula.

**Existence** says the integrand is there. In linear-algebra terms the stochastic integrals against $W$ span the whole space of square-integrable martingales on $\mathcal{F}^W$. Nothing lives outside the span.

**Uniqueness** is the half people skip, and the half that prices things. Suppose two integrands $H$ and $K$ both represented $M$. Then $\int (H - K) \, dW$ would be identically zero, and a short calculation with the Ito isometry forces $H = K$ almost everywhere. The coordinates are unique, the way coordinates in a basis are unique and coordinates in a merely spanning set are not.

That matters commercially. If two different integrands both replicated a claim, two desks could run two different perfect hedges at two defensible costs, and "the price" would not be a number. Uniqueness of the integrand is what turns a hedge into a valuation.

## From the theorem to the hedge ratio

The bridge is three steps and worth every one.

Fix a claim $X$ paying at time $T$ and work under the risk-neutral measure $Q$, the measure under which discounted asset prices are fair games. Why it exists, and why its existence is equivalent to no arbitrage, is the subject of [martingales and the risk-neutral measure](/blog/trading/math-for-quants/martingales-risk-neutral-measure-math-for-quants); the mechanics of moving to it are in [Girsanov's theorem](/blog/trading/math-for-quants/girsanov-change-of-measure-math-for-quants).

**Step one.** Define the discounted claim price $\tilde{V}_t = E^Q\!\left[e^{-rT} X \mid \mathcal{F}_t\right]$. By the tower property of conditional expectation, covered in [conditional expectation as projection](/blog/trading/math-for-quants/conditional-expectation-projection-math-for-quants), this is a $Q$-martingale. Not by assumption: by construction.

**Step two.** Girsanov changes the drift but not the filtration, so $\mathcal{F}_t$ is still generated by a Brownian motion, now $W^Q$. The representation theorem applies and hands us a unique adapted $H$ with

$$
\tilde{V}_t = \tilde{V}_0 + \int_0^t H_s \, dW^Q_s.
$$

**Step three.** The discounted stock satisfies

$$d\tilde{S}_t = \sigma \tilde{S}_t \, dW^Q_t,$$

and a self-financing portfolio holding $\Delta_t$ shares has discounted value moving as

$$d\tilde{X}_t = \Delta_t \, d\tilde{S}_t = \Delta_t \sigma \tilde{S}_t \, dW^Q_t.$$

Match that against the representation and read off

$$
\Delta_t = \frac{H_t}{\sigma \tilde{S}_t}.
$$

Start the portfolio at $\tilde{V}_0$ and it tracks $\tilde{V}_t$ to the last cent in every state of the world. That is replication, and the hedge ratio is the theorem's integrand over a known scale factor.

Notice what just happened to the recipe. If the claim is Markovian, so $V_t = v(t, S_t)$ for smooth $v$, Ito's lemma gives $H_t = e^{-rt} \sigma S_t \, v_x$ and the division leaves $\Delta_t = v_x$, the partial derivative. "Differentiate the price" is therefore not the definition of the hedge but a **special case** of the integrand, available only when a function of today's stock price exists to differentiate.

#### Worked example 1: replicating a \$25m call, three steps, exact to the cent

Continuous time hides the mechanism, so here is the finite version, where the theorem becomes visible arithmetic.

A stock trades at \$100 and moves up 10% or down 10% over each of three periods. Take the rate as zero. You have sold a call struck at \$100 on 250,000 shares, a notional of \$25,000,000. With factors of 1.1 and 0.9 and no interest the risk-neutral probability of an up move is exactly one half, and rolling the payoffs back through the tree makes the call worth \$7.475 per share, so you collect \$1,868,750 in premium.

![Three step binomial tree showing stock price, option value and hedge ratio at every node, with the up down up path highlighted and the hedge profit and loss on each leg summing to the payoff owed](/imgs/blogs/martingale-representation-hedging-math-for-quants-3.webp)

The tree above carries the stock price, the option value and the hedge ratio at each node, with one path picked out: up to \$110, down to \$99, up to \$108.90. At each node the hedge ratio is the discrete integrand, $\Delta = (V_u - V_d)/(S_u - S_d)$, the change in the claim over the change in the underlying.

| Leg | Stock move | $\Delta$ (exact) | $\Delta$ (4 dp) | Hedge gain per share | On 250,000 shares |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Start | \$100.00 | 21/40 | 0.5250 | value 7.475 | premium +\$1,868,750 |
| 1 | \$100 to \$110 | 21/40 | 0.5250 | +\$5.250 | **+\$1,312,500** |
| 2 | \$110 to \$99 | 331/440 | 0.7523 | −\$8.275 | **−\$2,068,750** |
| 3 | \$99 to \$108.90 | 89/198 | 0.4495 | +\$4.450 | **+\$1,112,500** |

Every gain there comes from the exact fraction, not the four-decimal display, so each row is a true identity: leg 2 is $\tfrac{331}{440} \times (-11) = -\tfrac{331}{40} = -8.275$ exactly.

Now walk the account. You start with \$1,868,750 of premium and buy $\tfrac{21}{40} \times 250{,}000 = 131{,}250$ shares at \$100, costing \$13,125,000, so you borrow \$11,256,250.

- After leg 1: \$1,868,750 plus \$1,312,500 is \$3,181,250, and the option there is worth \$12.725 a share, or \$3,181,250.
- After leg 2: \$3,181,250 minus \$2,068,750 is \$1,112,500, and the option is worth \$4.45 a share, or \$1,112,500.
- After leg 3: \$1,112,500 plus \$1,112,500 is \$2,225,000, and you owe \$8.90 a share, or \$2,225,000.

Total hedge P&L was +\$356,250, which added to the premium is exactly the payoff, on every one of the eight paths and not only this one.

That is not luck. At any node an up move is $S_u - S_0 = \tfrac{1}{2}(S_u - S_d)$, so the hedge gain is $\tfrac{1}{2}(V_u - V_d)$, and since $V_0 = \tfrac{1}{2}(V_u + V_d)$ the two add to $V_u$ identically. The tree is the representation theorem in finite terms: the option value is its starting value plus a sum of position times price change, and the position at each node is the only one that works.

One wrinkle. The exact position on leg 2 is 188,068.18 shares and on leg 3 it is 112,373.74. Round to whole shares and replication stops being exact, by about \$2 on leg 2. Hold that thought.

**The intuition:** the hedge ratio is not something you choose well. It is the only sequence of positions that could have turned the premium into the payoff.

## When the integrand does not exist

Everything above rested on one clause: the filtration is generated by *the* Brownian motion, the one the stock trades on. Break that and the machinery does not degrade gracefully. It stops.

Suppose the world carries a second independent Brownian motion $W^2$, driving volatility rather than the level, as in [jump-diffusion and stochastic volatility](/blog/trading/math-for-quants/jump-diffusion-stochastic-volatility-math-for-quants). A martingale on the enlarged filtration then needs two integrands:

$$
M_t = M_0 + \int_0^t H_s \, dW^1_s + \int_0^t K_s \, dW^2_s.
$$

Trading the stock buys you access to $W^1$ and nothing else. The $K$ term has no instrument behind it. There is no single $H$ representing the claim, so there is no hedge that replicates, so the price is not pinned down. This is the second fundamental theorem of asset pricing seen from the inside: the market is complete exactly when the risk-neutral measure is unique, and it is unique exactly when the traded assets span the filtration.

#### Worked example 2: two hedges, two prices, a \$2,500,000 gap

Make it arithmetic. A stock at \$100 over one month, rate zero, three outcomes rather than two: \$110 if it drifts up, \$90 if it drifts down, \$70 if it gaps. The gap is the second risk factor, and no amount of stock trading distinguishes it in advance from an ordinary large down move.

You have written a put struck at \$90 on 500,000 shares, a \$50,000,000 notional. It pays nothing at \$110 or \$90 and \$20 per share at \$70.

Risk-neutral measures must make the stock a fair game: $110q_1 + 90q_2 + 70q_3 = 100$, probabilities positive and summing to one. That is one equation short, and the family satisfying it is $q_1 = q_3 + \tfrac{1}{2}$, $q_2 = \tfrac{1}{2} - 2q_3$, with $q_3$ strictly between 0 and 0.25. The put is worth $20 q_3$ per share, anywhere from \$0 to \$5, and every one of those prices is arbitrage-free.

Two desks, two hedges:

- **Desk A hedges the diffusion.** Across the two ordinary states the payoff is zero and zero, so the hedge ratio is zero and the portfolio costs **\$0**. It replicates perfectly unless the gap happens, and then the desk owes \$20 a share on 500,000 shares, \$10,000,000, with nothing set aside.
- **Desk B super-replicates.** Short half a share and hold \$55 of cash per share: worth \$0 at \$110, \$10 at \$90, \$20 at \$70, so it covers the payoff in every state. It costs \$55 minus half of \$100, which is \$5 a share, or **\$2,500,000**.

![Two panels comparing a spanned market with two states and one Brownian motion against an unspanned market with a third jump state, showing the delta hedge that costs nothing and loses ten million against the super replicating hedge that costs two and a half million](/imgs/blogs/martingale-representation-hedging-math-for-quants-4.webp)

That diagram puts the two cases side by side. On the left, two states and one source of randomness, one hedge, one price. On the right, a third state the stock does not reach, and the arbitrage-free band runs the full \$0 to \$2,500,000. That gap on a \$50,000,000 book is not a rounding error or a calibration detail. It is the price of the claim being undetermined.

**The intuition:** incompleteness does not make the hedge worse. It makes the hedge a *choice*, and once it is a choice the price is an opinion, defensible anywhere inside the band. Desks close the gap by trading a second instrument, usually a listed option, which spans the second Brownian motion and restores completeness.

## The honest limit: existence is not computability

Existence results are famously quiet about how to find the thing. The standard proofs approximate the claim by stochastic exponentials and take limits, which tells you the integrand is there and hands you no formula.

The one constructive statement is the Clark-Ocone formula, $H_t = E^Q[D_t X \mid \mathcal{F}_t]$ with $D_t$ the Malliavin derivative. It still leaves you computing a conditional expectation of a derivative of a path functional, which for most exotics means a Monte Carlo inside a Monte Carlo. Existence is cheap; the integrand is not.

Reality then applies two further taxes. You cannot trade continuously, and you pay to trade at all.

#### Worked example 3: the \$50m book, rebalanced N times

You are short a three-month at-the-money call on 500,000 shares of a \$100 stock, a \$50,000,000 notional, at 25% volatility with the rate at zero. The Black-Scholes premium is \$4.983534 per share, \$4.98 to the cent, or \$2,491,767 on the position.

Over one rebalancing interval a delta-hedged option earns its gamma and pays its theta, and the two cancel only on average. The leftover per step is $\tfrac{1}{2} \Gamma S^2 \sigma^2 \Delta t (Z^2 - 1)$ for a standard normal $Z$, with mean zero and standard deviation $\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\Gamma S^2 \sigma^2 \Delta t$. Treating gamma as frozen and the steps as independent, $N$ of them accumulate to

$$
\text{sd of total hedging error} \;\approx\; \frac{\Gamma S^2 \sigma^2 T}{\sqrt{2N}}.
$$

At the money here, gamma is 0.03185311 per share per dollar, so $\Gamma S^2 \sigma^2 T$ is 4.977048 per share, or \$2,488,524 across the 500,000 share position. Those extra decimals are not decoration: round gamma to six places and the position figure lands eight dollars away. Assume too that each rebalance turns over roughly 5% of the notional, \$2,500,000, at a one-basis-point half-spread, so \$250 a go.

| Rebalances $N$ | Hedging error, 1 sd | As % of premium | Spread paid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 (monthly) | \$1,015,936 | 40.8% | \$750 |
| 13 (weekly) | \$488,040 | 19.6% | \$3,250 |
| 63 (daily) | \$221,695 | 8.9% | \$15,750 |
| 252 (4 a day) | \$110,848 | 4.4% | \$63,000 |
| 1,008 (16 a day) | \$55,424 | 2.2% | \$252,000 |

![Chart with hedging error falling as one over the square root of the rebalance count while cumulative spread paid rises linearly, crossing between 252 and 1008 rebalances on a fifty million dollar notional](/imgs/blogs/martingale-representation-hedging-math-for-quants-5.webp)

The chart above is the trade in one image. Error falls as ${1/\sqrt{N}}$ and cost rises as $N$, so the two lines have to cross.

Read the last two rows together. Going from daily to sixteen times a day takes the error from \$221,695 down to \$55,424, removing \$166,271 of standard deviation, and takes the spread bill from \$15,750 up to \$252,000, a certain \$236,250. You spend \$236,250 for sure to remove \$166,271 of maybe. Quadrupling the rebalance count halves the error every time, which is why the last few basis points of replication error are never worth buying.

**The intuition:** the theorem promises a perfect hedge in continuous time with no costs. Every dollar of gap between that and your P&L is what continuity and free trading would have been worth.

## Common misconceptions

**"The representation theorem tells you how to hedge."** It tells you a hedge exists and that there is exactly one, which are different claims, and the second is the valuable one. Every practical hedge ratio comes from a PDE, a tree, or a simulation. The theorem's job is to guarantee that whatever those converge to is the right answer and the only answer.

**"Completeness is a technical condition."** It is the entire reason a unique price exists. The second fundamental theorem makes completeness and uniqueness of the risk-neutral measure the same statement, so dropping completeness drops the price and leaves a band. Worked example 2 put that band at \$0 to \$2,500,000 on a \$50,000,000 book, the difference between a valuation and an argument.

**"Stochastic volatility just adds a parameter."** It adds a dimension to the filtration. One extra Brownian motion means one extra integrand, and the stock gives you no instrument to attach it to. You do not fix that by calibrating harder, you fix it by trading a second instrument that spans the new factor. Calibration only picks which measure inside the family you are using, and that is a choice dressed as a fit.

## In the interview room and on the desk

The question arrives plainly: **"Why does delta hedging work?"** Sometimes it is dressed as "where does the Black-Scholes delta come from?" or "what would you hedge a lookback with?", but it is the same question.

The weak answer differentiates the Black-Scholes formula. It is not wrong, and it ends the conversation, because the follow-up is "and if there is no formula?" and there is nowhere to go.

The strong answer runs in order. One: under the risk-neutral measure the discounted price of any claim is a martingale, by construction rather than assumption, since it is a conditional expectation of a fixed random variable. Two: the filtration is generated by a single Brownian motion, so the martingale representation theorem applies and produces an integrand. Three: the integrand is unique, and dividing it by the stock's diffusion coefficient gives the number of shares. Four: uniqueness is why the claim has a price at all, because two integrands would mean two perfect hedges at two different costs. Five, if they want it: when the claim is Markovian the integrand collapses to the partial derivative, so the recipe answer is a corollary rather than a rival.

The trap is treating completeness as an afterthought. Candidates mention it as a regularity condition and move on, which is backwards: it is the load-bearing assumption, and if you cannot say what breaks without it you have recited the theorem rather than understood it. The recovery is concrete. Name a second risk factor, stochastic volatility or a jump, observe that the representation now needs a second integrand with no instrument behind it, and note that the arbitrage-free price becomes an interval whose width you can compute. Quoting the super-replicating cost, the sub-replicating cost and the band between separates the candidate who has read Shreve from the one who has used it.

A second trap is claiming the theorem hands you the hedge. It hands you existence. Say so, mention Clark-Ocone as the constructive version, and note you would still price with a PDE or a simulation.

Jane Street, Citadel Securities and Optiver weight this heavily for options seats, and any desk quoting exotics pushes on the incompleteness follow-up, because it is where a trader's judgment starts and the textbook stops.
