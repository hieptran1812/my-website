> [!tldr]
> - **Claim.** The validity tests mechanistic interpretability (MI) uses do not pick out a unique explanation. Many incompatible circuits, interpretations, algorithms and localizations all pass them perfectly.
> - **Why it matters.** Papers say "we found *the* circuit". If the criteria cannot tell two different circuits apart, that definite article is not earned by the method.
> - **Most surprising result.** One MLP with two hidden layers of 3 neurons, trained on XOR, admits 85 perfect circuits, an average of 535.8 valid interpretations per circuit, and 159 perfect minimal mappings for just two candidate algorithms.
> - **The trend.** The count grows with width (median 38 to 910,000 circuit interpretations from $k=2$ to $k=5$) and fewer than 2% of networks have exactly one valid minimal mapping.
> - **Where it falls short.** Everything is exhaustive enumeration on toy MLPs and Boolean gates. Whether the ambiguity survives in transformers is argued, not shown.

Every circuit paper ends with a picture: a few heads and neurons wired together, each labelled with what it computes. Méloux and colleagues ask the question under all of those pictures: with the behavior and the validity criteria held fixed, is the answer unique?

Their answer, on networks small enough to enumerate every possibility, is a flat no. 

## Identifiability: borrowing a word from statistics

**The problem.** A method can be accurate and still tell you nothing about the thing you wanted to know. Statistics has a name for the failure: a model is *non-identifiable* when different parameter values produce exactly the same data distribution, so no amount of data can decide between them.

**Analogy.** Two witnesses agree on every observed detail of a crash, yet blame different cars. More observations of the same kind cannot settle it.

**Mechanism.** Formally, a parametric model $\{p_\theta : \theta \in \Theta\}$ is identifiable when equal distributions force equal parameters:

$$p_{\theta_1} = p_{\theta_2} \;\Longrightarrow\; \theta_1 = \theta_2 \quad \text{for all } \theta_1, \theta_2 \in \Theta$$

Here $\Theta$ is the set of allowed parameters and $p_\theta$ is the data distribution under $\theta$.

**Worked example (mine, not the paper's).** Fit $y = \theta_1 x + \theta_2 x$. Any pair with $\theta_1 + \theta_2 = 3$ explains data generated by $y = 3x$ perfectly. Both $(1, 2)$ and $(3, 0)$ have zero error, and the data cannot rank them.

**The transfer.** The paper defines *identifiability of explanation* the same way. Under fixed validity criteria, an MI strategy is identifiable if only one explanation passes. Two explanations are *incompatible* when they share the explanatory goal (here: the input-output behavior of a trained MLP) and the simplification strategy, but posit different computational abstractions. This rules out the easy escape of "explanatory pluralism", where different explanations answer different questions.

## What counts as a mechanistic explanation

The paper formalizes an explanation as a *computational abstraction* $\mathcal{A} = (S, \tau)$: a circuit $S$ (the *where*) and a mapping $\tau$ from neural states to the variables of a simple algorithm (the *what*).

![Figure 1 from Méloux et al. (2025): a circuit is a subgraph of the base network, and the mapping assigns high-level feature values to regions of neural activation space](/imgs/blogs/everything-everywhere-all-at-once-is-mechanistic-interpretability-identifiable-fig1.webp)

The authors' Figure 1 shows the two pieces. The middle panel is the circuit: the orange subgraph kept from the base network. The bottom strip is the mapping: feature $F_0$ splits one neuron's whole activation range into 0 and 1, $F_1$ only claims specific intervals, and $F_2$ carves a 2D activation space into three values.

| Object | Definition in the paper | Plain meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Circuit $S = (V_S, E_S)$ | A subgraph of the network graph $G = (V, E)$ with at least one input-to-output path | Which neurons and edges you keep |
| Mapping $\tau_j$ | A surjective map $\tau_j : \mathbb{R}^{\lvert V_j \rvert} \to \{f_0, \dots, f_m\}$ for each high-level variable $A_j$ | How to read a value off a group of neurons |
| Consistent mapping | Reading then computing equals computing then reading | The labels respect the network's own arithmetic |

Here $V_j$ is the neuron group for variable $A_j$ and $\{f_0, \dots, f_m\}$ its possible values; surjective means every value is reachable.

### The consistency condition

**Problem.** Any labelling of neurons is possible; we want labellings that track the computation. **Mechanism.** Take a variable $A_j$ with parents $PA_j$. Running the network from the parents' neurons and then reading $A_j$ must give the same value as reading the parents and running the algorithm:

$$\tau_j\big(\text{NN}(h_{PA_j})\big) \;=\; \text{Alg}\big(\tau_{PA_j}(h_{PA_j})\big)$$

where $h_{PA_j}$ is the activation vector of the parent neurons, $\text{NN}$ is the network's computation from those neurons to $V_j$, and $\text{Alg}$ is the algorithm's rule for $A_j$. This is a commuting diagram: both paths from the bottom-left corner to the top-right corner agree. The paper uses Boolean circuits, so every high-level variable is 0 or 1 and every rule is a logic gate.

## The two strategies and four questions

The authors sort MI methods into two families:

- **Where-then-what.** First find a circuit that reproduces the behavior (ACDC and attribution patching live here), then interpret its components.
- **What-then-where.** First hypothesize an algorithm, then search for neural subspaces causally aligned with its variables (Distributed Alignment Search lives here).

Each family has a *where* step and a *what* step, so uniqueness can fail in four places.

![A 2x2 grid: two strategies times where/what, each cell with its criterion and the XOR answer](/imgs/blogs/everything-everywhere-all-at-once-is-mechanistic-interpretability-identifiable-1.webp)

The grid above is the paper's whole argument: every cell gets the same answer on XOR. The rest of this post explains each count.

## The test bed: an MLP small enough to enumerate

Real circuit methods use heuristics because enumerating a transformer's subgraphs is intractable. On an MLP with layer sizes (2, 3, 3, 1), you can try them all.

![Figure 2 from Méloux et al. (2025): the XOR network stress-tested both ways, with perfect mappings for two candidate algorithms on top and many perfect circuits with multiple groundings below](/imgs/blogs/everything-everywhere-all-at-once-is-mechanistic-interpretability-identifiable-fig2.webp)

The XOR setup in Figure 2:

- Inputs $A$ and $B$ are 0 or 1 plus Gaussian noise of fixed standard deviation.
- The target is $C = \text{round}(A) \oplus \text{round}(B)$, where $\oplus$ is exclusive or.
- The network has a single logit output and two hidden layers of width 3.

No single neuron can compute XOR, so any explanation must route through intermediate gates. Four searches then run against the trained network.

| Search | Strategy | What it enumerates | Criterion tested |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Circuit search | Where-then-what | Every subgraph, run as a standalone network | Circuit error = 0 |
| Interpretation search | Where-then-what | Logic-gate labels for each neuron in a perfect circuit | Consistent mapping |
| Algorithm search | What-then-where | Boolean formulas for XOR of parse-tree depth at most 3 | IIA = 1 |
| Mapping search | What-then-where | Neuron subsets mapped to the algorithm's intermediate gates | IIA = 1 |

## Criterion 1: circuit error

**Problem.** A circuit should behave like the full model. **Analogy.** Pull fuses from a fuse box; if the house still works, the remaining fuses are sufficient. **Mechanism.** Remove every node and edge outside $S$, run the validation inputs, and count disagreements with the full model $g$.

$$\text{err}(S) \;=\; 1 - \frac{1}{\lvert \mathbf{x} \rvert} \sum_{x \in \mathbf{x}} \mathbb{1}\big[S(x) = g(x)\big]$$

Here $\mathbf{x}$ is the input set, $\lvert \mathbf{x} \rvert$ its size, $S(x)$ the circuit's output, $g(x)$ the full model's output, and $\mathbb{1}[\cdot]$ is 1 when its argument is true. A *perfect circuit* has error zero. With perturbed inputs the paper notes a KL-divergence version over logits, as in ACDC.

**Worked example.** XOR has four input patterns. A circuit that matches the model on $(0,0)$, $(0,1)$ and $(1,1)$ but flips $(1,0)$ has error ${1} - 3/4 = 0.25$. One that matches on all four has error 0.

**When it fails.** The criterion only asks about outputs, so two circuits sharing no hidden neurons can both score zero. On the XOR network, the exhaustive search finds **85 unique circuits with perfect accuracy**.

## Criterion 2: consistent mapping, and why one neuron has many meanings

**Problem.** Once you hold a circuit, you have to say what each neuron computes. **Mechanism.** The paper interprets neurons recursively, layer by layer. A neuron's parents already carry Boolean labels, so you list every combination of parent values, look at the activation the neuron produces for each, and enumerate every way to split those activations into a 0 side and a 1 side. Each clean split is a logic gate; each gate is a branch of a growing tree of candidate interpretations.

![A number line of one neuron's activations for the four input pairs, with three cut points yielding OR, A, and AND](/imgs/blogs/everything-everywhere-all-at-once-is-mechanistic-interpretability-identifiable-3.webp)

**Worked example (illustrative numbers, not from the paper).** Suppose a first-layer neuron $h$ outputs 0.1, 0.6, 0.7 and 1.4 for inputs $(0,0)$, $(0,1)$, $(1,0)$ and $(1,1)$. The figure above shows three valid cuts:

| Threshold | Inputs read as 1 | Gate label |
| --- | --- | --- |
| between 0.1 and 0.6 | 01, 10, 11 | OR |
| between 0.6 and 0.7 | 10, 11 | A (identity on the first input) |
| between 0.7 and 1.4 | 11 | AND |

Each threshold is a consistent reading of the same neuron. The next layer is then interpreted relative to each of the three parent readings, so the tree multiplies.

The search makes two concessions to avoid trivial over-counting. It ignores relabelling (swapping 0 and 1), assigning 1 to the higher interval by convention. It also only maps each gate to a *single* neuron, so it **undercounts**: distributed representations would add more interpretations.

**When it fails.** Consistency is a local test: each neuron must be separable given its parents. It never asks whether a threshold is natural, robust to noise, or shared with other tasks. On XOR, the 85 perfect circuits carry **an average of 535.8 consistent interpretations each**. Multiply and you get ${85} \times 535.8 = 45{,}543$ where-then-what explanations.

### A circuit with 24 readings

The appendix makes this concrete. For another XOR network (layer width 3, loss cutoff ${10}^{-3}$), an exhaustive search with no sparsity threshold finds 59 circuits; Figure 4 shows the twelve sparsest.

![Figure 4 from Méloux et al. (2025): the 12 sparsest perfect circuits in the example XOR network, kept neurons and edges in blue](/imgs/blogs/everything-everywhere-all-at-once-is-mechanistic-interpretability-identifiable-fig4.webp)

Across those 59 circuits the search finds 114,230 interpretations. Circuit 8 alone has 24, which the paper lists in its Table 1. In one reading, neuron (1, 0) is a NAND with separation boundary -0.003; in another it is a NOR at 0.321. Two of the resulting formulas:

- Interpretation 1: $\neg\big(\neg(A \land B) \rightarrow \neg(A \lor B)\big)$
- Interpretation 2: $\neg\big((\neg(A \land B) \lor \neg(A \lor B)) \rightarrow (\neg(A \land B) \rightarrow \neg(A \lor B))\big)$

Both compute XOR and both are consistent with every neuron in circuit 8, yet they describe different intermediate computations.

## Criterion 3: interchange intervention accuracy

The what-then-where family does not trust correlation. It tests whether intervening on a hypothesized variable has the same effect in the algorithm and in the network.

**Problem.** A label that merely correlates with a neuron could be coincidence; we want the neuron to *play the role* of the variable. **Analogy.** To check that a mixing-desk dial controls the bass, copy its setting from one song into another and listen. **Mechanism.** Run a *source* input and record a variable. Run a *base* input, but force that variable to the recorded value. Do the same in the network on the mapped neurons. Compare the two outputs.

![Two parallel lanes, algorithm and network, each running a source, reading a value, patching it into a base run, then comparing outputs](/imgs/blogs/everything-everywhere-all-at-once-is-mechanistic-interpretability-identifiable-2.webp)

The paper's Appendix A writes this out (adapted from Geiger et al., 2022). The low-level interchange is

$$\text{II}_{\text{low}}(N, b, s, V_k) \;=\; \big(N_{V_k \leftarrow N[s, V_k]}\big)[b, V_{\text{out}}]$$

where $N$ is the network, $N[s, V_k]$ the activations of neuron group $V_k$ on source input $s$, $N_{V_k \leftarrow v}$ a copy of $N$ with $V_k$ clamped to $v$ (a do-intervention), and $[b, V_{\text{out}}]$ means "run on base input $b$ and read the output neurons". The high-level $\text{II}_{\text{high}}(A, b, s, A_k)$ is the same operation on algorithm $A$ and variable $A_k$. Then

$$\text{IIA}(N, A, A_k, \tau) \;=\; \frac{1}{\lvert \text{Val}(A_{\text{in}}) \rvert^{2}} \sum_{b, s \,\in\, \text{Val}(A_{\text{in}})} \mathbb{1}\big[\text{II}_{\text{high}}(A, b, s, A_k) = \text{II}_{\text{low}}(N, b, s, V_k)\big]$$

with $\text{Val}(A_{\text{in}})$ the set of all input combinations. IIA is the fraction of (base, source) pairs on which the counterfactual outputs agree. A perfect mapping has IIA = 1 for every intermediate variable; a *minimal* perfect mapping has no other perfect mapping with larger images (set inclusion-wise).

**Worked example.** Take the algorithm $\text{XOR} = \text{AND}(\text{OR}(A,B), \text{NAND}(A,B))$ and intervene on OR. Source $s = (0,0)$ gives OR = 0. Base $b = (1,0)$ normally outputs 1, but with OR forced to 0 the algorithm outputs $\text{AND}(0, 1) = 0$. If the network, with the mapped neuron patched from the source run, also outputs 0, this pair agrees. XOR has 4 inputs, so there are ${4}^{2} = 16$ pairs, and IIA = 1 means all 16 agree.

**When it fails.** IIA scores one algorithm against one mapping. It never compares two algorithms against each other, and it does not penalize a network for hosting several. The paper also notes two practical gaps: IIA is usually estimated on sampled inputs rather than all pairs, and it assumes the neurons for distinct variables are disjoint, $\tau^{-1}(x) \cap \tau^{-1}(y) = \emptyset$, which superposition violates.

### The algorithm and mapping counts

The algorithm search enumerates Boolean formula parse trees built from AND, OR and negation. Assuming each neuron encodes identity, AND or OR, a network of depth $d$ can only implement formulas of depth at most $d$, and for $d = 3$ there are **56 XOR-equivalent formulas**.

In the Figure 2 example the authors test only two candidates by hand and find **159 perfect minimal mappings**, with both algorithms perfectly aligned. In the appendix network, 4 of the 56 formulas produce valid mappings:

| Formula | Expression | Distinct minimal mappings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $\neg(A \land B) \land (A \lor B)$ | 2 |
| 2 | $\neg((A \land B) \lor \neg(A \lor B))$ | 2 |
| 39 | $\neg((A \land B) \lor \neg(A \lor B)) \land (A \lor B)$ | 4 |
| 40 | $\neg(((A \land B) \lor \neg(A \lor B)) \lor \neg(A \lor B))$ | 4 |

Formula 1's two mappings put $A \lor B$ on neuron (1, 2) or on neuron (1, 0), with $\neg(A \land B)$ on neuron (1, 1) both times. Same algorithm, two locations, both at IIA = 1. That is question four of the grid, answered with a concrete pair of neurons.

## Scaling it up: does the ambiguity go away?

One XOR network could be a fluke. Section 4 repeats the whole pipeline across seeds while varying width and task count. Setup: $n$ two-input gates $L_1, \dots, L_n$, an MLP with layer sizes $(2, k, k, n)$, trained on noisy binary samples until mean squared loss drops below $n \times 10^{-3}$.

Two constraints keep enumeration feasible, and both make the counts **lower bounds**:

- Circuits must contain two inputs and one output per target gate.
- Only circuits with sparsity above 0.3 (more than 30% of components removed) are tested, so the larger, denser circuits are never counted.

![Figure 3 from Méloux et al. (2025): circuit interpretations and perfect minimal mappings per network, against width k (left) and number of trained gates n (right), log scale](/imgs/blogs/everything-everywhere-all-at-once-is-mechanistic-interpretability-identifiable-fig3.webp)

**Width (left panel).** From $k = 2$ to $k = 5$, the median number of explanations grows from 38 to 910,000 in the circuit-first method and from 8 to 3,700 in the algorithm-first method. Fewer than 2% of trained networks contain exactly one valid minimal mapping, and **no network contains exactly one circuit interpretation**.

**Multiple tasks (right panel).** With $k = 3$ and $n$ from 1 to 6 gates trained in parallel, the count decreases significantly ($p = 0.05$) up to 4 tasks, then plateaus.

| Experiment | Variable | Effect on number of explanations | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Width | $k = 2 \to 5$ | Circuit-first median 38 to 910,000; algorithm-first 8 to 3,700 | §4.1.1 |
| Multi-task | $n = 1 \to 6$ | Decreases up to 4 tasks, then not significant | §4.1.2 |
| Loss cutoff | ${10}^{-1} \to 10^{-6}$ | Modest drop in algorithms at $\leq 10^{-5}$; mappings per algorithm unchanged | App. D.4 |
| Input noise | none vs $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.1)$ | Algorithm-first unchanged; fewer circuits, many more interpretations | App. D.5 |
| Training skew | joint entropy 0.8 to 2.0 bits | Algorithm-first unchanged; skew raises circuit count | App. D.6 |
| Larger model | MNIST 0 vs 1 MLP | At least 3,209 valid circuits if any exist | §4.3 |

### Training dynamics do not save us

Maybe the slack of a non-zero final loss creates the ambiguity? Appendix D.4 sweeps the cutoff from ${10}^{-1}$ to ${10}^{-6}$. Algorithms drop modestly but significantly at cutoffs of ${10}^{-5}$ or lower; mappings per algorithm do not move; circuits only drop at the loosest cutoff (0.1). The authors also find multiple abstractions in *randomly initialized* networks that happen to implement a gate, with no training at all.

![Table 3 from Méloux et al. (2025): average abstraction counts without and with Gaussian input noise, with Welch's t-test p-values](/imgs/blogs/everything-everywhere-all-at-once-is-mechanistic-interpretability-identifiable-fig5.webp)

Noise cuts both ways (Table 3). Training with $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.1)$ input noise roughly halves the number of circuits (52.3 to 28.6) but raises interpretations per circuit from 2,270 to 16,400, so total interpretations climb from 107,000 to 285,000 (all $p \lt 0.001$). The algorithm-first numbers barely change: 2.70 vs 2.56 algorithms, 9.86 vs 9.89 total mappings. My guess (not the paper's claim) is that noise widens the gaps between activation clusters, leaving room for more valid thresholds.

### The MNIST lower bound

Section 4.3 sidesteps enumeration on a real dataset. An MLP with layers (784, 128, 128, 3, 3, 3, 1) is trained on MNIST digits 0 and 1. The authors split it into a front (784, 128, 128, 3) and a tail (3, 3, 3, 1), feed the training set through the front, and enumerate circuits in the tail on those intermediate activations: **3,209 valid circuits**.

If the front has no valid circuit, neither does the full network; if it has one, each tail circuit extends it, giving at least 3,209.

## What the paper suggests we do about it

Section 5 is a discussion offering three ways forward and a warning:

| Stance | What it means | Example from the paper |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Uniqueness is not required | Judge explanations by predictivity and manipulability, not by being "the" truth | Wu et al. defend DAS against the interpretability-illusion critique on exactly these pragmatic grounds |
| Add a tie-breaker | Prefer the sparsest circuit | The authors reject Occam's razor alone: in their experiments simplicity does not single out one explanation |
| Stricter criteria | Full causal-model consistency: every low-level state accounted for, every excluded component justified | Faithfulness (Hanna et al., 2024) checks that removed components really do not matter |
| Many criteria at once | Validate one explanation with several independent tests | The inner interpretability framework of Vilas et al. (2024) |

The warning is §5.3: identifiability may be unachievable. Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics posit different entities and make identical predictions; philosophers call this contrastive underdetermination. Combined with complexity results showing that circuit queries are intractable (see [the computational complexity of circuit discovery](/blog/paper-reading/ai-interpretability/the-computational-complexity-of-circuit-discovery-for-inner-interpretability)), MI might be underdetermined in principle.

### What this changes in practice (my extrapolation)

The paper gives no checklist, but I would adopt these habits:

1. Report the number of circuits that pass your threshold, not just the one you drew. If your method returns one, say that it returned one, not that one exists.
2. When you interpret a component, state the threshold and test at least one neighbouring threshold. If the label flips, the label is a choice.
3. For what-then-where work, test rival algorithms against the same network. A perfect IIA for your hypothesis is weak evidence if a rival also scores 1.
4. Treat circuit hypotheses as statistical claims. [Hypothesis testing the circuit hypothesis](/blog/paper-reading/ai-interpretability/hypothesis-testing-the-circuit-hypothesis-in-llms) is one concrete way to do that.

The same non-uniqueness shows up in feature dictionaries: [sparse autoencoders do not find canonical units](/blog/paper-reading/ai-interpretability/sparse-autoencoders-do-not-find-canonical-units-of-analysis) makes a parallel argument about SAE latents.

## Critique

**What is strong.** Shrinking the network until enumeration is exact replaces "our heuristic found one answer" with "here is every answer". The lower-bound framing is honest, and one counterexample network suffices to break a uniqueness claim. Appendix C collects phrasing like "the curve circuit" and "fully recovers the circuit" from well-known papers, which shows the unicity assumption is real and not a straw man.

**What is weak.**

- **The *what* space is tiny and the counts are still huge, which cuts both ways.** Boolean gates with threshold readings is a deliberately permissive language. A reader can fairly ask how many of the 535.8 interpretations per circuit a human would accept as natural.
- **Single-neuron mappings.** The interpretation search ignores distributed representations, so it undercounts. But real MI often uses directions (DAS, SAEs), and the paper never measures identifiability in that richer space beyond noting it.
- **Toy-to-real gap.** §5.4 argues that if the problem vanishes at scale, someone must show why. That is a fair burden shift, but it is a burden shift, not evidence. The MNIST result covers circuits only, and only as a conditional lower bound.
- **Loose bookkeeping.** Figure 2's bottom panel prints a count of 25 while the text reports 45,543 interpretations, and Section 3's 85 circuits differ from Appendix B's 59. Different seeds or conventions may explain it; the paper does not say.

**Missing ablation.** I would want the same counts under the stricter criteria the authors recommend: faithfulness on excluded components, or full causal consistency. If those collapse 45,543 explanations to a handful, the paper's practical message becomes "use stricter criteria" rather than "identifiability may be impossible".

**What would change my mind.** If a study applied faithfulness or full causal-consistency criteria to these same toy MLPs and found exactly one surviving explanation in most networks, I would read this paper as a critique of today's metrics rather than of MI's goal. Conversely, if a transformer circuit like IOI turned out to have many equally faithful rivals under exhaustive search on a reduced model, the toy-scale caveat would stop mattering.
